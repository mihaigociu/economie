# Lesson 3 — The Two Values: Experiential vs Exchange

> **Part I · Where economies come from** · Source: *Talking to My Daughter About the Economy*, ch. 2
> Motifs in play: 🩸 the blood that dries up · 💎 diamonds & water · 🧲 the market's solvent

---

## The hook: the gift that money killed

In 1970 a British researcher, Richard Titmuss, noticed something that should have been impossible according to every economics textbook.

Britain collected its blood entirely through *voluntary donation* — no payment. The United States relied much more on *paying* donors. The textbook prediction is obvious: pay for a thing and you'll get more of it. Yet Britain's unpaid system produced blood that was more plentiful, safer, and less wasted than the American paid system. And when some places tried *introducing* payment to boost supply, donations sometimes **fell**.

How can offering people money to do something make *fewer* of them do it? Every "incentives" chapter you've ever read says the arrow points up. Here it pointed down.

Hold that puzzle. By the end of this lesson you'll see that it isn't a glitch. It's a window into the deepest thing markets *cannot see* — and therefore the deepest reason you must never let the market be the only judge of worth.

---

## Two kinds of value, and why the difference is everything

Everything of worth to a human being has value in (at least) two utterly different senses. Confusing them is the single most common mistake in public argument about the economy — and the one the market makes by design.

- **Experiential value** — what something is worth *in the living of it*: in having it, doing it, being part of it. The warmth of a friend showing up when you're low. A swim in the sea at dawn. Reading to a child. The satisfaction of doing a job *well* because it's yours. This value is felt, not paid. It usually has **no price at all** — and often *cannot* have one without ceasing to be what it is.

- **Exchange value** — what something fetches when it's bought and sold: its **price**. This is the only value that appears in a shop, a payslip, or GDP. It is real and it is useful. But it is a *thin slice* of the whole — and a slice that, as we're about to see, can eat the rest.

> Oscar Wilde defined a cynic as *"a man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing."* That line is the whole lesson in eleven words. A market, left to itself, is a perfect Wildean cynic.

---

## The market's blind spot — and why it matters

Here is the crucial claim, stated plainly:

> **The market can perceive only exchange value. Experiential value is, to the market, literally invisible.**

A price tag can attach to a bottle of wine, but not to the evening you drink it with someone you love. GDP records the €30 restaurant meal (Lesson 1) but is blind to your grandmother's identical meal cooked from love. The market isn't *lying* about this — it simply has no organ for perceiving experiential value, the way a camera has no organ for perceiving smell.

That would be harmless if the two values stayed in their own lanes. The danger — the engine of half this course — is that **in a market society (Lesson 1), exchange value doesn't stay in its lane. It spreads.** More and more of life gets a price. And here's the sting: *the very act of pricing something can corrode or destroy its experiential value.* The measurement changes the thing measured.

---

## Why pricing can *destroy* value: three real experiments

The blood puzzle isn't a one-off. When economists and psychologists went looking, they found the pattern everywhere. The mechanism has a name — **crowding out**: an external price crowds out the internal motive that was doing the real work.

**1. The blood donation (Titmuss, 1970).** Donating blood for free is an act of solidarity — an experiential value: *I am the kind of person who helps strangers.* Pay for it, and you reframe the same act as a cheap job. The high-minded reason evaporates; all that's left is a poor wage, so some walk away. **Adding money subtracted meaning.**

**2. The Israeli daycare fine (Gneezy & Rustichini, 2000).** A group of daycare centres had a problem: parents arriving late at pick-up. They introduced a small fine for lateness. Late pick-ups promptly went *up*. Why? Before the fine, arriving late meant *guiltily imposing on the teachers* — a moral, experiential cost. The fine converted that into a *price*: a small fee you could simply *buy*, guilt-free. "I'm sorry" became "I'll pay for it." **The fine didn't add to the moral cost; it replaced it — with a cheaper one.** And worse: when the centres later *scrapped* the fine, lateness stayed high. Once a relationship has been turned into a transaction, you can't always turn it back.

**3. Paying children to read.** Pay a child per book and, at first, they read more. But you've quietly taught them that reading is a *chore worth money*, not a pleasure worth having. Stop paying — as you eventually must — and they often read *less* than children who were never paid, because the intrinsic joy has been overwritten by an extrinsic price that's now gone. **You bought short-term compliance with long-term love of reading.**

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"But hold on — sometimes paying people obviously works. If I pay someone to fix my roof, I get a roof. You're cherry-picking heart-warming cases."*
>
> Fair, and important. Money works beautifully — is often *the right tool* — where the activity has *little experiential value to begin with*. Nobody has a sacred inner relationship to fixing your roof; it's a job, price it and be done. The danger is specific and predictable: it strikes wherever the thing was being powered by an **internal motive — solidarity, guilt, love, pride, duty** — that money can *substitute for and thereby switch off*. The skill I'm teaching you is to tell the two situations apart, because a market society keeps applying the roofer logic to the blood-donor cases, and is genuinely puzzled when it backfires.

## The oldest clue: diamonds and water 💎

Long before Titmuss, Adam Smith — the patron saint of markets — stumbled on the same crack and couldn't quite close it. He called it the **paradox of value**:

> Water is essential to life; you'd die in days without it. Diamonds are useless — you can't eat, drink, or shelter under one. Yet water is nearly free and diamonds cost a fortune. **The thing of immense value to living has almost no price; the near-useless bauble commands a king's ransom.**

Read through our two-values lens, the "paradox" dissolves. Water has enormous **experiential value** (life itself) but, where it's abundant, almost no **exchange value** (no scarcity, no price). Diamonds have trivial experiential value but high exchange value (artificially scarce, heavily marketed). Price tracks scarcity and marketing, *not* worth-to-a-human. **The market's price and a thing's real value to human life are two different quantities that only sometimes coincide** — and mistaking one for the other is how a society ends up rich in priced things and poor in the unpriced ones that actually make life worth living.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Okay, but if price isn't real value, how are we supposed to compare things and decide? At least a price is a number everyone can see."*
>
> Exactly the seduction — and exactly the trap. A price *is* wonderfully convenient: one number, universally legible, easy to add up. That convenience is why exchange value keeps winning the argument and colonising life. But convenient is not the same as complete. The mature position isn't "prices are evil" — it's: *prices are one instrument on the dashboard, brilliant for some questions (how scarce is this? how do I allocate the roofer's time?) and blind, even dangerous, for others (is this friendship, this river, this vocation being served?).* An economist worth trusting always knows which of the two she's looking at. A cynic — and an untended market — can't tell the difference.

---

## Why this belongs at the foundation of the course

You might ask why I've spent a whole lesson on a philosophical-sounding distinction. Because it is the hinge between Part I and everything after:

- It explains **GDP's blindness** (Lesson 1, and sidebar S2 later): GDP is a sum of exchange values, so it counts the forest cut down and sold, and records zero for the forest left standing and loved.
- It is the hidden cost of the **market society** (Lessons 1 and 5): turning *everything* into a commodity isn't just efficient reallocation — it steadily converts experiential value into exchange value, and some of it is destroyed in the conversion, never to return.
- It sets up the **ecology** finale (Lesson 24): a stable climate has near-infinite experiential value and, until we invent one, no price — so the market treats the planet as free, and bills us later.
- And it will let us judge **technofeudalism** (Lesson 23): when a platform harvests your attention and friendships and turns them into its exchange value, whose experiential value is being mined?

Keep the two values in both hands from here on. Whenever someone says "the market has spoken," your first reflex should now be: *spoken about what — exchange value? Then what did it fail to hear?*

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

| Term | In one honest sentence |
|------|------------------------|
| **Experiential value** | What something is worth in the living of it — felt, usually priceless, often destroyed by being priced. |
| **Exchange value** | What something fetches when bought and sold: its price; the only value the market and GDP can see. |
| **Crowding out** | When an external price/reward substitutes for and switches off an internal motive (solidarity, guilt, love), reducing the behaviour. |
| **Commodification / marketisation** | Turning something that wasn't for sale into something that is — which can convert (and partly destroy) experiential value into exchange value. |
| **Paradox of value** | Adam Smith's puzzle — water cheap, diamonds dear — resolved once you see price tracks scarcity, not worth-to-life. |
| **The market's blind spot** | The market perceives only exchange value; experiential value is invisible to it by construction. |

## The takeaway

Everything worth having has value in two incompatible senses: **experiential** (what it's worth in the living) and **exchange** (its price). The market — and GDP, its scorekeeper — can see only the second. That's tolerable until exchange value spreads, as it does in a market society, and starts *pricing* things whose worth was made of solidarity, love, guilt, or pride — because the act of pricing can *crowd out and destroy* the very value it meant to capture. The blood dries up. The parents stop feeling guilty. The child stops loving books. **Money is a solvent: it dissolves other values into its own, and some of what dissolves doesn't come back.** The economist's job is never to worship the price or to despise it, but always to know *which value she's looking at* — and what the price cannot hear.

Next lesson: we take up the thread we started early in Lesson 2 and follow it properly — **debt came first, and money grew out of it.** There was never a tidy world of barter that money came to rescue.

## 🧠 Interruption Quiz — Lesson 3

*Answer before you peek. Open the key only once you've committed.*

**Q1 (multiple choice).** In Titmuss's finding, introducing payment for blood donation sometimes *reduced* donations because:
- (a) the payments were too small to matter
- (b) payment reframed an act of solidarity (experiential value) as a poorly-paid job, **crowding out** the internal motive
- (c) people didn't trust that they'd actually be paid
- (d) there was suddenly a shortage of donors for unrelated reasons

**Q2 (classify).** Label each as primarily **experiential** or **exchange** value:
1. The $2,000 market price of a diamond ring
2. The feeling of being proposed to with that ring
3. A litre of tap water in a city where water is abundant
4. Teaching your little cousin to swim on a Sunday

**Q3 (true/false — and say *why*).** *"A higher price always means a thing is more valuable."* True or false — use the diamonds-and-water paradox to justify your answer.

**Q4 (explain the mechanism — open).** In the Israeli daycare study, a fine for late pick-ups made lateness *increase*, and scrapping the fine didn't fix it. Explain **both** results using the two-values framework.

**Q5 (apply — open).** A city proposes paying residents a small cash bonus for every hour they volunteer at the local food bank, hoping to increase volunteering. Using this lesson, predict what could go wrong, name the mechanism, and say under what conditions paying *would* have been safe.

<details>
<summary><strong>▶ Answer key & explanations (open after answering)</strong></summary>

**Q1 — (b).** *Concept: crowding out.* The money didn't add to the reasons to donate; it *replaced* a strong internal motive (solidarity, moral self-image) with a weak external one (a small wage), so some donors withdrew. This is the signature of crowding out.

**Q2 — 1: exchange · 2: experiential · 3: experiential · 4: experiential.** *Concept: the two values.* Note items 1 and 2 attach to the *same object* (the ring) — one is its price, the other its meaning. And item 3 echoes the water paradox: huge experiential value, near-zero exchange value where abundant.

**Q3 — False.** *Concept: paradox of value.* Water is essential (immense experiential value to life) yet cheap where abundant; diamonds are near-useless yet dear. Price tracks **scarcity and marketing**, not worth-to-a-human. So a high price signals that something is *scarce and/or well-marketed*, which only sometimes lines up with real value. *Full credit requires the scarcity-not-worth point, not just "false."*

**Q4.** *Concept: crowding out + irreversibility.* (1) *Why lateness rose:* before the fine, arriving late carried a **moral/experiential cost** — guilt at imposing on teachers. The fine converted that into a **price** parents could simply pay, guilt-free ("I'll pay for it" replaces "I'm sorry"), and a small price bought a lot of extra lateness. The fine *substituted for* the moral cost with a cheaper monetary one. (2) *Why scrapping it didn't help:* once the relationship had been reframed as a transaction, removing the fine didn't restore the old moral cost — you can't easily un-commodify a relationship. *Full credit addresses both the substitution and the irreversibility.*

**Q5.** *Concept: crowding out; when money is safe.* **What could go wrong:** volunteering is powered by experiential value (solidarity, civic pride, "I'm a good neighbour"). A cash bonus can *crowd that out*, reframing it as underpaid work; committed volunteers may feel insulted or lose their internal motive, and net volunteering could **fall** — and might not recover if the bonus is later withdrawn (irreversibility). **Mechanism:** crowding out of intrinsic/moral motivation by an extrinsic price. **When paying is safe:** where the activity has little experiential value and is treated as a straightforward job from the outset (the "roofer" case) — e.g. paying professional staff to run logistics, rather than paying the volunteers whose whole motive is the giving. *Strong answers name crowding out AND correctly identify the low-experiential-value condition under which payment is fine.*
