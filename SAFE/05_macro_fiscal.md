## Executive summary (read this first)

- Romania's SAFE allocation: **€16.68 bn** signed 21 May 2026 (loan agreement). Second largest after Poland's €43.73 bn.
- Loan terms publicly stated by PM Bolojan and corroborated by Italian-comparator and Romanian-press sources: **~3% fixed rate, 45-year maturity, 10-year grace period, repayments from 2035 over ~35 years, 15% (~€2.5 bn) pre-financing on entry into force, availability window to 31 December 2030**.
- Headline subsidy: Romania's market alternative is a EUR-denominated sovereign bond yielding roughly **5.5–6.5%** (vintage May 2026; RON 10Y at ~7.09%, German Bund ~3.1%, RO EUR-2033 reference issue traded with a 6.375% coupon). Versus SAFE at ~3%, the spread saved is **~250–350 bps**, or **~€420–580 m per year** in interest cost during the grace period, and a **nominal lifetime saving of roughly €15–21 bn** over the 45-year horizon (illustrative annuity math in §1.4).
- Conditionality is non-trivial. SAFE disbursement is milestone-based, runs under the EU's general budget rule-of-law conditionality regime (Reg. 2020/2092), and requires at least 65% EU/EEA-EFTA/Ukrainian content. Tranches stop if milestones slip — Romania has direct PNRR experience (€869 m suspended in 2025, €231 m forfeited over magistrates' pensions milestone) of what that looks like in practice.
- Fiscal context is the binding constraint. Romania is **in EDP since 2020**, deficit 9.3% of GDP in 2024 falling to ~7.65–8% in 2025 (vintage Jan 2026 reports), targeting 6.2–6.5% in 2026 and 5.5–5.8% in 2027. Debt rises from ~55% (end-2024) toward **63% by 2027** and **~70% by 2030 (IMF)**. **Fitch BBB-/Negative** (affirmed 14 Feb 2026), **S&P BBB-/Negative** (affirmed Apr 2026), **Moody's Baa3/Negative**.
- The **National Escape Clause for defence** (NEC, activated July 2025 for 15 MS) allows up to 1.5% of GDP/year of defence overrun outside the Stability Pact corrective path through 2028. **Romania did not request the NEC in the original cohort** (15 in July 2025; Germany Oct 2025; Austria Feb 2026) — material because Romania is already under EDP and the political optics of asking for *another* derogation were judged unwelcome. SAFE debt itself, however, counts as ESA-2010 general-government debt: every euro disbursed adds to Romania's Maastricht debt stock. SAFE is *cheaper* debt; it is not *off-balance-sheet* debt.
- Opportunity cost: SAFE is mostly **additional** to prior defence plans (Romania was already targeting 2.5–3.5% of GDP on defence), but partly **substituting** for civilian capex. The **A8 motorway and parts of A7 were originally in the PNRR**, lost their PNRR funding in 2025 due to delays, and have been moved into SAFE under the dual-use mobility category. That is reshuffling, not new money.
- Comparator picture is decidedly mixed. Poland claims **89% of its €43.7 bn flows to domestic industry** and German share only **0.37%**. Romania, by contrast, has Rheinmetall (Germany) sitting on roughly **€5 bn or ~52% of its €9.5 bn defence-procurement envelope** as prime/joint contractor. The structural variable is industrial scale: Poland has PGZ; Romania does not.
- **Counterfactual:** absent SAFE, Romania would likely have (a) issued more EUR-denominated debt at ~5.5–6.5% to fund a smaller, slower programme; (b) drawn more on US FMF/FMS and bilateral export credits, plausibly with worse terms but better political cover at home; (c) postponed the A7/A8 sections and the corvette restart. The headline financial saving is real (~€15–21 bn nominal over 45 years) but the *capability* delta is smaller because acquisitions would still have happened on a longer fuse.

## 1. SAFE loan economics for Romania

### 1.1 The instrument

SAFE — *Security Action for Europe*, Council Regulation adopted 27 May 2025, in force 29 May 2025 — authorises the Commission to borrow up to **€150 bn on capital markets** and on-lend to participating member states. The structure is back-to-back/diversified (closer to NextGenerationEU than to the strict back-to-back of the original EFSF model): the Commission issues plain "EU-Bonds" — there is no defence-labelled instrument — and pools proceeds. SAFE-driven issuance is one of the reasons the Commission **raised its 2026 funding target by €20 bn to €180 bn**, and the H1 2026 target by €10 bn to €100 bn (Commission press release 6 May 2026).

Why "no defence-labelled bonds"? The Commission considered, and rejected, the option of a separately-marketed "Defence Bond" or "Security Bond" line. The decision (announced in the SAFE borrower-investor pages) was to fund SAFE through the **single branded EU-Bond/EU-Bills curve**. The reasoning is liquidity and pricing: a single, deep curve allows tighter pricing than fragmented sub-labels (the experience of NextGenerationEU green bonds, which traded at a small premium to the conventional EU-Bond curve, was instructive but not large enough to justify a defence sub-label). For the member-state borrower, this means SAFE pricing is a derivative of the *general* EU-Bond curve, not of any defence-risk-specific spread.

By end-2026 the Commission expects to have **roughly €1 trillion of bonds outstanding** — making it a larger borrower than Belgium and the Netherlands combined, and the **fifth-largest sovereign-equivalent issuer in Europe**. EU-Bonds have rallied relative to Bunds since 2022 (when the EU-Bund spread peaked near 70 bp following the NGEU surge); by 2025 the spread had compressed to ~40 bp at the 10-year point. This compression is the source of most of the "passthrough subsidy" that flows to high-yield-curve member states like Romania.

The Commission's borrowing programme is rated:
- **AAA / stable** (DBRS Morningstar — confirmed for 2026 issuance).
- **AA+ / stable** (S&P, Fitch).
- **Aaa / stable** (Moody's).

The pricing reference is the **AAA euro-area sovereign curve** (effectively the Bund curve, plus a roughly 30–75 bp EU-bond premium that has widened/tightened with EU issuance volume).

Empirical 2025 syndication snapshots (Commission communiqués):
- 9th syndication, Oct 2025: 7-year tap at mid-swap **+34 bp**, re-offer yield **2.889%**. 15-year new issue at mid-swap **+75 bp**, re-offer yield **3.666%**.
- 10th syndication, Nov 2025: 5-year tap at re-offer yield **2.527%**.
- 4th syndication, early 2025: 30-year (Oct-2052) at mid-swap **+126 bp**, re-offer yield **3.859%**; 3-year tap at re-offer yield **2.283%**.

By Apr–May 2026 the **Euro Area 10Y benchmark was ~3.47–3.55%**; EU-Bonds at the long end (20–30y) would price in the **3.7–4.0%** range. The blended weighted-average cost of EU funding for a 45-year on-lend is therefore plausibly **~3.4–3.8%** at issuance — meaning Romania's quoted ~3% rate is **either** a fixed rate set when SAFE-supporting bonds were first issued (when long yields were 25–50 bp lower), **or** a notional rate net of any short-end discount on the average tranche profile. The 3% number is consistent with the EU's funding cost and not a subsidy *from the EU's borrowing side*; the subsidy is the **spread between EU borrowing rates and Romania's own**.

### 1.1a How EU borrowing translates into SAFE pricing — the mechanics

The chain of transmission from Commission borrowing on capital markets to Romania's loan rate has four steps:

**Step 1 — Commission bond issuance.** The Commission auctions EU-Bonds (long-dated, syndicated) and EU-Bills (short-dated, auctioned). Each tranche prices off mid-swap, with a spread that has varied between mid-swap +20 bp (short end, 2025 conditions) and mid-swap +75–125 bp (long end, 30-year). Re-offer yields in 2025 ran from ~2.3% (3-year) to ~3.9% (30-year).

**Step 2 — Pooling.** Proceeds from all EU-Bond issuance feed into a single "diversified funding" pool — not back-to-back-matched to specific countries' disbursements. This is the same architecture the Commission has used since the 2023 unified funding approach.

**Step 3 — Calculation of the SAFE on-lend rate.** The Commission calculates a *cost-recovery* base rate that reflects the weighted-average cost of the funding pool, plus a small administrative margin. The methodology (drawn from EFSF/ESM/EFSM precedent) involves a "base rate" + "service fee" + (optionally) a "guarantee commission" — though under SAFE there is no MS-guarantee since the EU budget underwrites the issuance.

**Step 4 — Application to the loan.** Romania's loan agreement specifies how the cost-recovery rate is locked in: either as a single fixed rate at the time of the loan agreement signing (which appears to be the Italian and likely Romanian practice — hence the "~3%" public statement), or as a series of tranche rates re-calibrated each year as funds are drawn.

For Romania, given:
- The loan agreement was signed on 21 May 2026.
- The EU's weighted-average cost of funding in mid-2026 is roughly 3.4–3.8% (blend of 2y EU-Bills ~2.4%, 5y EU-Bonds ~2.5%, 10y ~3.9%, 30y ~3.9%).
- Bolojan and other officials quote "approximately 3%" or "3% fixed".

The most likely interpretation is that the Romania SAFE loan is **structured with several rate-locked tranches** corresponding to the funding-pool composition at the time of each drawdown, with the **headline 3% being a near-term short-end-weighted approximation**. The effective lifetime cost is therefore plausibly **closer to 3.3–3.5% averaged across the 45-year envelope** once long-dated funding flows through.

This nuance matters for the NPV calculation: a 30 bp shift in the average rate (3.0% → 3.3%) reduces the lifetime nominal saving from ~€18 bn (vs 6.0% Romania benchmark) to ~€15 bn — still large, but it dilutes the headline subsidy by ~15%.

### 1.3 The headline subsidy — Romania's market alternative

The honest comparator is **Romania's EUR-denominated sovereign yield** at comparable maturity — not the RON yield. Mixing RON and EUR overstates savings because Romania pays a currency-risk premium on RON debt that has nothing to do with SAFE.

Reference points (all vintage May 2026 unless noted):
- Romania **RON 10Y**: 7.09% (20 May 2026, Trading Economics).
- Romania **EUR-2033 benchmark** (XS2689948078): coupon 6.375%, traded yield in the 5.5–6.5% area through 2025–early 2026.
- Romania EUR-2031, 2034 issues: yields in similar 5.5–6.0% range earlier in 2025.
- German **Bund 10Y**: ~3.1% (vintage early 2026, Reuters/CNBC for the March yield-spike episode).
- **Euro-Area 10Y benchmark**: 3.47–3.55% (May 2026).
- **EU-Bonds 10Y**: ~3.9–4.0% (estimated using +40 bp EU-Bund spread).

So Romania's **EUR spread over Bund** is roughly **240–340 bp** in May 2026; its spread over EU-Bonds (the underlying SAFE funding cost) is **~150–250 bp**. **Romania's spread over the SAFE on-lend rate of ~3% is ~250–350 bp**, which is the headline subsidy.

The subsidy is "headline" because, ignoring shadow costs (conditionality, lock-in to 65%-EU procurement, milestone risk), the cash saving on the same notional is:

| Reference market rate (Romania EUR sovereign) | Spread vs SAFE 3% | Annual interest saving on €16.68 bn (yr 1-10, interest-only) |
|---|---|---|
| 5.5% (optimistic, post-rating-stabilisation) | 250 bp | **~€417 m/yr** |
| 6.0% (mid; current EUR-2033 trading area) | 300 bp | **~€500 m/yr** |
| 6.5% (stress; post a further notch downgrade) | 350 bp | **~€584 m/yr** |

For perspective, **€500 m/year is ~0.15% of Romanian GDP** (2025 GDP ~€330 bn). It is **roughly equal to one-third of Romania's annual debt-service line in the 2025 budget**, and it is **comparable in scale to the entire annual revenue of the Ministry of Culture**.

### 1.5 Conditionalities — what can stop the money

1. **Milestone-based disbursement.** Article 12 of the SAFE Regulation makes payments contingent on fulfilment of conditions set out in Romania's National Defence Investment Plan and the loan agreement. This is the same architecture as the RRF: each tranche is unlocked against milestones (procurement signature, delivery, joint-procurement participation, industrial-participation thresholds).
2. **Rule-of-law conditionality (Reg. 2020/2092).** SAFE explicitly applies the **general regime of conditionality for the protection of the Union budget**. This is the same instrument that froze a slice of Hungary's cohesion envelope. A European Parliament question of Q1 2026 (E-001383/2026) flagged that SAFE Article 12 leaves the scope of the conditionality test "without clearly specifying", which has invited concerns about politicisation — but the legal architecture exists and Romania's existing PNRR experience is the closest precedent (€869 m suspended June 2025, €231 m forfeited Q1 2026 over the magistrates'-pensions milestone, recovered subsequently).
3. **65% origin rule.** End-product value: at least 65% must originate in EU/EEA-EFTA/Ukraine. Maximum 35% can come from third countries, with limited exceptions for in-flight contracts and feasibility-justified components. Exceeding 35% non-EU content invalidates the eligibility of the contract for SAFE financing — and therefore the disbursement.
4. **Anti-concentration rule.** No more than 60% of the €150 bn envelope can go to the three largest recipients. Poland (€43.73 bn) + Romania (€16.68 bn) + France (€16.22 bn) = **€76.6 bn = ~51%**, within the 90 bn (60%) cap, so the rule is **not currently binding** but constrains future re-allocations.
5. **European Semester linkage.** Country-Specific Recommendations addressing rule-of-law-adjacent issues (judicial independence, anti-corruption, public procurement, AML) feed into the conditionality assessment. Romania has multiple open CSRs on procurement transparency and judicial reform.
6. **No formal NEC linkage.** SAFE participation does **not** require the member state to have activated the National Escape Clause for defence under the Stability and Growth Pact. Romania has not activated NEC; SAFE participation does not depend on it.

The practical risk: SAFE disbursement does **not** carry the rule-of-law sword to the same political extent that cohesion-policy money does, but it is *legally available*. The bigger empirical risk is milestone slippage — the contracts ledger (Task 2) suggests several Romanian SAFE programmes have ambitious 2027–2030 delivery dates that the Mangalia shipyard and the Lynx local-production plan may struggle to meet.

## 2. Romanian fiscal context and rating impact

### 2.1 The deficit and debt trajectory

| Metric | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 (est) | 2026 (proj) | 2027 (proj) | 2030 (IMF) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| General-government deficit, % of GDP | 6.7 | 9.3 | 7.65–8.0 | 6.2–6.5 | 5.5–5.8 | ~5 (IMF) |
| Gross government debt, % of GDP | 48.9 | 54.8–55 | ~59 (Fitch) | ~60.4 (S&P) | ~63 | **~70 (IMF)** |
| GDP growth, % | 2.1 | 0.8 | ~0.7–1.0 | 1.5–2.2 (EC) / 2.8 (BNR) | ~2.0 | — |

Sources: Fitch (14 Feb 2026), S&P (Apr 2026), Commission Spring 2025 forecast, IMF Article IV Nov 2025 conclusion, BNR June 2025 FSR, OSW Commentary 12 Jan 2026, ING Think 2025–26.

The trajectory has been deteriorating since 2020:
- Pre-pandemic debt was 35.2% of GDP (2019).
- COVID shock pushed it to 46.9% by end-2020 (deficit 9.3%).
- 2021–23 deficits in the 6.5–7.2% band.
- 2024 deficit re-peaked at **9.3% — the highest in the EU**.
- 2025 measures (Bolojan government, July/September packages) target ~6% in 2026 but the **IMF November 2025 Article IV is sceptical**: it projects the deficit narrowing only gradually to ~5% by 2030, with **debt-to-GDP continuing to rise to close to 70%**. IMF says **additional ⅔ percent of GDP/year of consolidation from 2027** is needed to put debt on a stable path below 60%.

### 2.2 Defence spending — current and committed

- 2024 actual: **~2.24% of GDP** (per Politico Pro, Feb 2025).
- 2025: **target 2.5% of GDP**.
- 2026 budget: Ministry of Defence requested **57 bn lei in direct allocations (~2.81% of GDP)** plus 112 bn lei in commitment appropriations for long-term programmes (F-35, Abrams). Final allocation pending parliamentary process.
- 2030 commitment: **3.5% of GDP "effective" + 1.5% on enabling factors (cybersecurity, dual-use infrastructure)** — i.e., the **NATO 5% framework** announced at the Hague summit June 2025.
- President Nicușor Dan (elected May 2025) and PM Bolojan have both endorsed the 3.5% effective + 1.5% enabling trajectory.

Note: the **5% NATO target is partly accounting**: the 1.5% "enabling" tier counts dual-use infrastructure (motorways, rail, ports, energy, cybersecurity, healthcare-system surge capacity). SAFE finances exactly the right basket: the A7/A8 motorways are dual-use; the cyber and electronic-warfare programmes also qualify under the 1.5% tier; the 9.6 bn defence-procurement envelope counts toward the 3.5% effective tier.

### 2.3 Does SAFE debt count toward EU fiscal rules?

**Yes.** SAFE loans are general-government liabilities under ESA 2010 and add to Maastricht gross debt. Eurostat's debt-and-deficit methodology counts them as such (analogous to RRF *loans*, distinct from RRF *grants* which do not add to Maastricht debt).

The €16.68 bn principal, if fully drawn by end-2030, **adds roughly 4 percentage points to Romania's debt-to-GDP ratio** (against a 2030 GDP of ~€420 bn). This is consistent with the Cristian Socol estimate quoted in defence-industry.eu ("Debt-to-GDP impact: +4 percentage points") and is the marginal increment that IMF's 70% 2030 projection is implicitly capturing.

What SAFE does is make the debt **cheaper** (saves ~€500 m/year in interest during the grace period vs market borrowing), and **longer** (45-year maturity vs typical Romanian Eurobond 7–15 years). The shape of the debt curve smooths the rollover; the **stock** still goes up.

### 2.4 The National Escape Clause — and why Romania did not activate it

The **NEC for defence** was activated by the Council on 8 July 2025 for **15 member states** (Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Greece, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Portugal, Slovakia, Slovenia) and later for Germany (Oct 2025) and Austria (Feb 2026).

The NEC allows up to **1.5% of GDP/year of defence spending** to be excluded from the Stability and Growth Pact's net-expenditure ceiling and the EDP corrective path, for **four years (2025–2028)**.

**Romania did not activate NEC.** This is a politically significant omission given Romania's defence-spending commitments. The likely reasons:
- Romania was already deep in EDP with the highest deficit in the EU. Requesting *another* derogation would have been a bad market signal.
- Romania could finance defence via SAFE (cheap, long-dated debt) rather than via NEC-protected national expenditure (still raises measured deficit). SAFE provides the cash; the deficit/debt accounting still hits, but the rating-agency narrative is "EU-supported defence investment" rather than "fiscal slippage".
- Politically, NEC activation would have required signalling commitment to deficit reduction in *non-defence* lines, which the coalition was already finding hard.

The interaction is that the NEC is **excluded from the corrective path** in budget surveillance; SAFE-financed defence outlays are not. So countries that activated NEC and use SAFE have **both** a higher net-expenditure ceiling **and** cheap EU loans. Romania has only the second.

### 2.4a Why SAFE debt is on Romania's books

A common confusion is whether SAFE loans count as "EU debt" or "national debt". The answer is unambiguous in ESA 2010 accounting:

- **SAFE loans add to the borrowing member state's Maastricht debt** (analogous to RRF *loans*).
- They do **not** add to the EU's own institutional debt at the consolidated EU-government level (because they are on-lent in full).
- The Commission's bond-issuance liability is matched by an equal claim on the member state, so the EU level shows a zero net asset/liability position.

For Romania, this means:
- Every euro drawn under SAFE shows up in the gross-debt-to-GDP ratio that Eurostat publishes twice yearly (April and October) and that drives Fitch/S&P/Moody's calculations.
- The interest cost in any given year is what Romania actually owes the EU under the loan agreement (3% during grace, ~3% on the annuity afterwards), not the EU's underlying borrowing cost.
- If Romania defaulted on SAFE, the Commission would have recourse against the EU budget (in practice, against the headroom between the Own Resources ceiling and actual expenditure) and against all member states — but this is a backstop, not a substitute for Romania's direct obligation.

The corollary: SAFE does not free Romania from any of the fiscal discipline expected under the EDP or the SGP. It is **cheaper, longer-dated borrowing**, not free money.

### 2.5 Rating-agency reactions

**Fitch (14 February 2026):** Affirmed **BBB-/Negative**. Key sentences (paraphrased from Romania-Insider Feb 2026):
- Debt was ~59% of GDP end-2025, **above the BBB current median of 56%**, projected to rise to 63% in 2027.
- 2026 deficit expected to fall by ~2pp from 2025's ~8%, but deficits "will remain among the highest in the BBB category".
- Risks: "visible tensions within the coalition could challenge the adoption of further measures" + weak growth (<2% potential to 2027).
- **No explicit mention of SAFE in the analytical text.** Fitch treats SAFE as additional debt at favourable terms — financially constructive, but not enough to flip the outlook.

**S&P (April 2026):** Affirmed **BBB-/Negative**.
- Net debt-to-GDP rising to 60.4% by 2026, then +1.3pp/year through 2029.
- Deficit path: 6.5% (2026), 5.5% (2027), 4.3% (2029) — vs. government's 3.2% target → S&P is more pessimistic than the government.
- Concerns: lack of post-2026 consolidation measures, tax-collection efficiency, social impact of consolidation, coalition tensions.
- **SAFE not explicitly factored as a rating risk.**

**Moody's:** Baa3/Negative (affirmed earlier; standing affirmation 2025).

**Common thread:** All three agencies are at the lowest investment-grade notch with negative outlook. **A one-notch downgrade puts Romania into sub-investment-grade ("junk")** territory, with mechanical consequences:
- Forced selling by investment-grade-only mandated funds.
- Removal from major investment-grade indices (Bloomberg Global Agg, ICE BofA IG).
- Sovereign-yield blowout of ~100–150 bp historically observed in CEE downgrades.
- Bank-sector knock-on (Romanian banks' sovereign exposure mark-to-market hit).

**SAFE's rating impact is ambiguous.** Constructively, it lowers the marginal cost of new defence-related debt and signals EU support. Negatively, it adds €16.68 bn to the debt stock — 4pp of GDP — which is the *exact* concern the agencies have been flagging. As of the May 2026 cohort of rating actions, neither S&P nor Fitch viewed SAFE participation as enough to either trigger or avert a downgrade. The bigger driver in both agencies' commentaries is **structural fiscal consolidation** (VAT-gap closure, pension reform, public-wage trajectory), not defence.

### 2.6 BNR (Central Bank) commentary

- **Financial Stability Report, June 2025** (the most recent at time of writing): Identifies "fiscal sustainability" as the dominant macro risk. Notes "impact of higher defence spending" as one consideration but does not single SAFE out.
- **NBR Board Decision, 15 May 2026:** Held policy rate (data does not specify level but signals cautious unwinding).
- **BNR Governor (May 2026):** Publicly stated Romania is "far from debt default risk" — verbal floor-setting amid market noise. This is part of the floor management that has kept the RON 10Y at 7.09% rather than spiking further.

### 2.7 Fiscal Council (Consiliul Fiscal) commentary

The Romanian Fiscal Council's *Preliminary Opinion* on the 2026 budget framework and successive comments (Dec 2025; mid-2025 ahead of the corrective package) take a guardedly positive line on SAFE:

> "The EU's SAFE program can help both the budgetary correction and the strengthening of Romania's defence capacity."

The Council emphasises that:
- The 7% deficit target for 2025 is "highly ambitious" and requires both expenditure and revenue measures.
- Reducing the deficit cannot rely exclusively on expenditure cuts.
- Defence-spending increases must be balanced with broader fiscal consolidation.
- The corrective package adopted in July–September 2025 *might* avert a sovereign downgrade — the Council expressed "cautious optimism" in December 2025.

Read between the lines: the Council sees SAFE as **fiscal lubricant** (cheap money substituting for expensive sovereign issuance) rather than as **fiscal cost** (the headline addition to debt). It is not raising independent alarms about SAFE itself.

### 2.8 IMF Article IV (concluded 7 November 2025)

Key directorial statements:
- "Critical to fully execute the planned fiscal consolidation in 2025–26 followed by additional adjustment in the medium term."
- Reform package, if fully executed, expected to reduce primary deficits by **1¼ pp of GDP (2025)** and **2 pp (2026)**.
- Overall deficit narrowing to ~6% in 2026 and ~5% by 2030.
- **Debt-to-GDP rises to ~70% by 2030 on current trajectory.**
- Additional consolidation of ~⅔% of GDP/year required from 2027 to stabilise debt at ~60%.
- The Article IV does **not** prominently address defence-spending increases or SAFE specifically. Defence is implicit in the spending forecasts.

The IMF's silence on SAFE is itself a signal: the Fund treats SAFE as a fiscally-neutral refinancing instrument — favourable terms relative to market issuance, but not as a substitute for primary-balance discipline.

## 3. Opportunity-cost / crowding-out analysis

### 3.1 Is SAFE additional or substituting?

**Mostly additional, partly substituting.** The decomposition:

**Additional (~€10–12 bn of the €16.68 bn):**
- The defence-procurement envelope of ~€9.5 bn (21 programmes) was already partially planned in MApN's multi-year procurement schedule, but on a **slower** timeline using national budget appropriations. SAFE pulls forward and tops up by accelerating the May-2026 contract-signing deadline.
- The €2.8 bn Ministry of Interior / security-services envelope is largely new — the funding category did not exist outside the SAFE/CEF dual-use framework before 2025.

**Substituting (~€4–5 bn):**
- **A7 motorway (Pașcani–Suceava–Siret)**: 5 lots of the A7 Moldova motorway were originally PNRR-funded. The PNRR renegotiation completed July 2025 shifted these into a mix of PNRR-grants-only (5 lots) and **non-PNRR funding** including SAFE-eligible dual-use-mobility (some segments).
- **A8 motorway (Mocta–Iași–Ungheni)**: Lost PNRR funding in 2025 due to delays. **Now expected to be funded under the Cohesion Policy or under SAFE.** The Romania-Insider July 2025 reporting is explicit on this.

So roughly **€2 bn of A7/A8 motorway spend is reshuffled** from PNRR to SAFE, not new money in net terms. The total "strategic highways" envelope under SAFE is ~€4.2 bn per the Task 1/Task 2 research, of which perhaps half is true substitution.

### 3.2 PNRR / cohesion absorption implications

- Romania's revised PNRR (post-July 2025 renegotiation) totals **€21.4 bn (€13.6 bn grants + €7.8 bn loans)**, down from €28.5 bn initially. The reduction is largely projects deemed undeliverable by the **31 August 2026 RRF deadline**.
- Romania has only **completed ~17% of the 2021–2027 Cohesion Policy envelope** (€5.3 bn out of €31 bn) by mid-2025 — well below the EU average absorption pace.
- **€6.3 bn in PNRR projects at risk of missing the 2026 deadline** (Romania Journal, 2026).
- The third PNRR payment had **€869 m suspended** in June 2025 over unmet milestones (substantially recovered by Q4 2025 after corrective action).
- **€231 m permanently lost** in early 2026 over the magistrates'-pensions milestone (Milestone 215) — funds Romania has to compensate from state budget.

**The implication for SAFE:** Romania's bureaucratic absorption capacity is the binding constraint, not the availability of money. Adding €16.68 bn of SAFE to the absorption queue — on top of €13.6 bn unspent PNRR grants and €25+ bn unspent cohesion-policy money — risks **administrative congestion**. Each programme has its own milestones, payment requests, audit trails, and Commission-counterpart interlocutors. The MApN, the Min. of Finance, the PM's Chancellery, and the Min. of Investment and European Projects (MIPE) are now juggling four overlapping disbursement regimes (RRF, ERDF/CF, SAFE, plus Modernisation Fund and others). The risk is not that SAFE goes unused, but that SAFE comes at the cost of cohesion absorption — exactly the wrong trade for an economy where infrastructure capex absorption is the structural bottleneck.

### 3.3 Civilian capex foregone — the real economic cost

Compared to a counterfactual where Romania did **not** access SAFE and the defence-procurement envelope was reduced by 50%, the civilian capex that is *not* foregone (because SAFE substitutes for self-financed defence borrowing) is:
- Direct interest savings of ~€500 m/year during the grace period.
- Releases ~€10 bn of debt-issuance space (Romania's total annual gross financing need is ~€25 bn; SAFE pre-financing displaces ~10% of that for two years).

What is foregone:
- The **Mangalia shipyard cost premium**: Romania is paying ~€350 m/OPV vs Bulgaria's ~€251.5 m/OPV for similar designs — a ~€200 m premium across two vessels to support shipyard revival. That money could have gone to other sectors but for the political imperative of saving Mangalia jobs.
- The **30% price-hike claim** (Minister Miruță, March 2026): IFV programme moved from €2.98 bn to €3.34 bn, OPVs from €700 m to €836 m, diver-intervention craft from €57 m to €84 m. If these increases reflect supplier discipline being eroded by SAFE's compressed timelines, the **counterfactual saving** of running a longer, more competitive process might have been ~€500 m–€1 bn across the whole portfolio.

### 3.4 Is SAFE money "double-counted" against PNRR?

Not formally — the EU's accounting rules prohibit double-counting an investment against more than one EU instrument. But the **economic** double-counting risk is real:
- The A7/A8 motorway segments shifted from PNRR to SAFE represent **reshuffling, not net additionality**.
- The same domestic-industrial-participation commitments (~50% local content) appear in multiple SAFE projects and in PNRR R&D&I programmes.
- The fiscal headroom argument — "SAFE saves Romania €500 m/year in interest, freeing room for healthcare/education" — is weakened if some of that headroom is *required* to compensate for forfeited PNRR money (e.g., the €231 m magistrates'-pensions loss).

A clean accounting frame: net of the PNRR shifts and assumed price escalation, SAFE delivers perhaps **€10–11 bn of true additional defence capability** for **€16.68 bn of new debt** — implying an effective absorptive efficiency of ~65%, in the same range as historical Romanian EU-fund absorption rates.

### 4.4 EU-wide third-country share

- **South Korea (Hanwha, Hyundai Rotem, LIG Nex1)**: Largest non-EU/EEA share. Poland's CHUNMOO/Chunmoo-on-Jelcz programme and possibly K2 tank tranche-2 are the main vehicles. **Estimated aggregate $3–5 bn (~€3–4.5 bn)** across SAFE, almost all via Poland.
- **United States (via EU subsidiaries)**: Lockheed (HIMARS, F-35 sustainment — but most F-35 is *not* SAFE-eligible because of FMS routing), Raytheon (Patriot components), Boeing (mil-air). **Estimated €2–4 bn aggregate**, mostly through subsidiaries in EU MS.
- **UK** (SDP holder, so up to 65% participation): BAE Systems (CV90 via Hägglunds AB which is BAE-owned but EU-domiciled), MBDA UK, Babcock. Counted toward the EU 65% via Swedish/Italian/French subsidiaries. **Estimated €3–5 bn**.
- **Norway** (SDP): Kongsberg NASAMS, Naval Strike Missile (in Romania's portfolio at ~€207 m). **Estimated €1–2 bn aggregate**.

### 4.5 The Poland counterfactual — what 89% domestic share looks like

Poland's headline that **89% of its €43.7 bn SAFE allocation flows to Polish industry** (and just 0.37% to Germany) is the most striking comparator data point. Worth unpacking:

- **PGZ (Polish Armaments Group)** is the integrating prime: a state-controlled holding with ~30 subsidiaries covering everything from small arms (Fabryka Broni "Łucznik") to artillery (Huta Stalowa Wola for Krab/Borsuk), MLRS/missiles (Mesko for Piorun, Grom; co-production of Korean CHUNMOO with Hanwha via Jelcz vehicles), radar/EW (Wojskowe Zakłady Elektroniczne), military aviation MRO (WZL #1 Łódź), machine guns and mortars (Zakłady Mechaniczne Tarnów), and procurement integration (Cenzin).
- **Implementation routing**: BGK (Bank Gospodarstwa Krajowego, the state development bank) takes the SAFE loan, channels funds through the **Armed Forces Support Fund (FWSZ)** established 2022. This off-balance-sheet vehicle has been Poland's primary defence-investment workhorse since the 2022–23 procurement surge.
- **Korean dimension**: Hanwha Aerospace produces Chunmoo launchers; PGZ subsidiary Jelcz mounts them on locally-made vehicles. The "Polish share" of a Chunmoo-on-Jelcz round therefore includes the launcher (Korean) **but excludes the launcher cost from the 89% claim** — i.e., the headline 89% measures *value-add inside Poland*, not *contractor of record*.
- **Capacity strain**: PGZ's defence-sector trade union sent a public letter to PGZ management in autumn 2025 raising concerns that the order pipeline outstrips production capacity. Roughly half of the SAFE money flows *outside* PGZ (to private Polish defence firms and to foreign primes' local subsidiaries), which means the 89% number is consistent with substantial foreign-prime activity *inside* Poland.

For Romania, the closest analogue would be **ROMARM** as integrating prime. But ROMARM:
- Has roughly 6,000 employees across 13 subsidiaries (Cugir, Carfil, Tohan, Plopeni, Sadu, etc.) vs PGZ's ~17,000.
- Specialises in small arms, ammunition, and light-vehicle subcomponents — **not** AFVs, naval combatants, or air-defence systems.
- Has no design-authority status on any major SAFE-scope platform.

The structural conclusion: Romania **cannot** replicate Poland's 89% domestic share even with twice the political will, because the industrial capacity does not exist. Romania's domestic-share target of 40–50% (claimed) and probable actual outcome of 30–40% is the **upper bound** given current ROMARM capacity. The remainder must flow through foreign-prime local subsidiaries (Automecanica Mediaș for Rheinmetall, Mangalia administered by Rheinmetall, Victoria propellant JV) or via offset agreements. These can deliver real economic value (jobs, tax revenue, supply-chain integration) but **the prime contractor of record is foreign**, which shapes the political optics and the dependence-risk calculus.

### 4.5a Stress-testing the Polish 0.37%-German claim

Prime Minister Tusk's claim that **"well over 80% of SAFE funds will benefit Poland"** and that **German industry will receive only 0.37%** deserves three layers of unpacking.

**Layer 1 — Definitional:** The 0.37% figure measures *direct German prime-contractor receipts*. It excludes:
- German subcomponents inside Polish-prime products (e.g., MTU engines in Krab/Borsuk vehicles).
- German subcomponents inside non-German foreign primes' products (e.g., Rheinmetall ammunition components in Korean Chunmoo deliveries).
- Diehl, Hensoldt, ZF, KAVAN, etc. content delivered as second/third-tier suppliers, where the contract relationship is with PGZ but the value-chain ultimately runs through Germany.

A more honest measure — the **German economic footprint** in Polish SAFE outlays — is plausibly **3–8%** rather than 0.37%, with the higher figure if you count all upstream German content. This is still much lower than Romania's likely 30–50% German share, but it is not "negligible".

**Layer 2 — Contextual:** The 0.37% claim was made in a Polish domestic-political context where the opposition argued the prior 2022–24 procurement surge had been *too* US/Korea-weighted, leaving German primes under-served. Tusk's point was that under SAFE he was restoring an **EU-leaning balance**, not that German primes had been excluded. The 0.37% is therefore an artefact of timing: the big German-prime opportunities (Leopard tank tranche, IRIS-T/Patriot AD) had already been placed in 2022–24, leaving SAFE to fund the *next* layer (artillery, MLRS, anti-drone) where Polish primes and Korean firms had the inside track.

**Layer 3 — Comparator implication for Romania:** Even Poland's 0.37% direct-German claim is not directly comparable to Romania's situation, because:
- Poland's SAFE plan was published *after* Poland had already placed many major awards. Romania's SAFE plan **is the primary procurement vehicle** rather than a top-up on prior awards.
- Romania has no PGZ equivalent. Its national-track primes must necessarily lean on European multinationals.
- Romania's geography (Black Sea, Moldova adjacency) places different platform requirements than Poland's (Suwalki Gap, Belarus border) — driving more naval and air-defence content where European primes dominate.

### 4.6 The Hungary side-story — what happens when conditionality bites

Hungary's case in May 2026 is the live test of SAFE's rule-of-law and anti-corruption guardrails. The sequence:

- **December 2025**: Orbán government submits Hungary's €16.22 bn SAFE plan. Plan includes large allocations to 4iG (Orbán-linked telecoms and IT group with growing defence portfolio) and other politically-connected entities.
- **Early 2026**: Commission gives **initial sign-off** but withholds final loan-agreement signature.
- **April 2026**: Hungary holds national election. Péter Magyar's Tisza party wins (against Orbán's Fidesz). New government takes office.
- **22 April 2026**: Magyar's transition team announces it will **re-review the entire SAFE plan** for corruption-concentration risks. Bloomberg reports Magyar seeking to "cut Orban-linked firms" from SAFE.
- **Status May 2026**: Hungary is **the only remaining applicant country awaiting final SAFE approval**. The €16.22 bn could be reduced, re-allocated, or — in the extreme — partially withheld.

For Romania, the Hungary case provides a useful comparator: it confirms that the **Commission can and will hold up disbursement** when political-economy concerns arise, and that **government changes can re-open negotiations**. Romania's political situation in May 2026 (coalition tensions, no-confidence-vote dynamics around the loan signing) means a future government could in principle re-negotiate, though the structural commitments to Rheinmetall and others are sticky once contracts are signed.

### 4.7 Country comparator commentary

- **Poland's "89% domestic, 0.37% German" claim** is striking and probably accurate given PGZ's industrial scale. It depends on the Polish defence industry being able to absorb €43.7 bn at the planned cadence, which is itself an open question — PGZ's union sent a letter raising capacity-utilisation concerns. The model is: SAFE finances Polish primes; Polish primes integrate foreign systems (Korean CHUNMOO, US HIMARS-adjacent components); the headline economic flow stays in Poland.
- **Romania's structural disadvantage** vs Poland is the absence of an integrating prime. ROMARM is the rough analogue but smaller and less capable. The 50% local-content target relies on **Rheinmetall-led offsets and JVs** (Automecanica Mediaș, Victoria propellant JV, Mangalia shipyard administration), which means **Romania's "domestic" share is, in significant part, Rheinmetall's local subsidiaries**. The economic value-capture is therefore real but routed through a German prime rather than a national champion.
- **Italy's loan structure** ("5-year fixed-rate at 3%") is reported with apparent precision but is poorly explained in source coverage — most likely means: a tranched drawdown over 5 years (2026–2030), each tranche at the then-fixed EU funding cost rounded near 3%, with the 45-year maturity envelope applying to the latest disbursement. Romania's structure is plausibly identical.
- **Czechia's CV90 example** is the cleanest case of SAFE topping up a pre-existing contract: the 246-vehicle CV90 deal was signed in 2023 (€2.5 bn) and Czechia is using SAFE to either backfill financing or extend the order. The 40%-Czech-industry requirement was already in the underlying contract; SAFE simply provides cheaper money.
- **Hungary's pause** post the 2026 election is the clearest illustration of the rule-of-law conditionality teeth. Péter Magyar's incoming government is reassessing the plan submitted by Orbán in December 2025 because of corruption-concentration concerns around 4iG (Orbán-linked). The Commission has not yet finalised Hungary's loan agreement.

## 5. Counterfactual sketch

### 5.1 What would Romania likely have bought / borrowed / from whom without SAFE?

**The "no-SAFE" world** would have featured:

1. **Defence procurement: smaller, slower, more US-weighted.**
   - The 9.6 bn of national-track procurement would still have happened, but staggered over 7–10 years rather than concentrated by the May 2026 contract-signing window. Cumulatively, perhaps 70–80% of the same nominal value.
   - More of it would have been **US FMF/FMS-routed** (continuing the F-35, Abrams, Patriot pattern). The reason: when SAFE-eligibility is not the constraint, Romania's procurement gravity tilts toward Washington — the FMS route is well-trodden, US Senate financing options exist, and the political-deterrence narrative favours US kit. The Korean route (Hanwha K9/K2/Redback) would have been a stronger competitor for the IFV slot than it was under SAFE, where the 65%-EU rule and SDP gating worked against Hanwha at the RFI stage.
   - The Rheinmetall share would likely have been **30–35% rather than 52%**, with US primes and Korean firms taking the displaced share.

2. **A7/A8 motorways: slower, possibly cancelled segments.**
   - The A8 segments that lost PNRR funding in 2025 would likely have been **deferred 5–10 years** under national-budget funding, with parts possibly de-scoped.
   - The A7 already had committed funding lines (PNRR grants + national budget); slippage would be moderate (2–4 years).

3. **MoI / national-security envelope: largely unfunded.**
   - The €2.8 bn for MoI/security-services dual-use is largely net new. Without SAFE, much of this would not have happened or would have been spread over 10+ years.

4. **Sovereign borrowing: more, at higher rates.**
   - To finance the residual ~70% of the defence-procurement envelope at market rates, Romania would have needed to issue approximately **€7–10 bn additional EUR-denominated debt at 5.5–6.5%**, plus matched RON debt at 7%+.
   - Net interest-cost differential vs the SAFE counterfactual: **~€300–500 m/year in steady state**, compounded.

5. **Capability outcome:** Approximately **70–80% of the SAFE-funded capability** by 2030–2032, with **6–18 months delay** for most major programmes, and a **different supplier mix** more weighted toward US/Korean primes.

### 5.2 Net-present-value sketch

Constructive framing: SAFE is a financing instrument layered on top of acquisitions that would, to a large extent, have happened anyway. The NPV question is therefore:

> **NPV = (financial saving from cheaper borrowing) + (capability value of acceleration) − (cost of supplier lock-in and reduced competition) − (administrative and absorption-capacity costs)**

#### 5.2.1 Financial-saving NPV (Section 1.4 numbers re-stated)

PV of debt-service saving vs RO EUR-mid (6.0%) at 5% discount: **~€7 bn** over 45 years.

In nominal terms: **€18 bn** of saved interest over 45 years.

This is the cleanest, most defensible component of the NPV.

#### 5.2.2 Capability-value of acceleration

- Bringing forward ~30% of the procurement envelope by 12–24 months has value, particularly for high-readiness items (air defence, ammunition, IFVs) in a Black Sea front-line context.
- Difficult to quantify in money terms; the standard convention is to assume 5–15% of programme value as deterrence/readiness premium for 1-year acceleration.
- For €5 bn of accelerated programmes: **€250 m – €750 m present value of acceleration**.

#### 5.2.3 Cost of supplier lock-in and reduced competition

- The Lynx IFV programme was selected **without competitive trials** against CV90/Redback/K21. If the market price for a competitive IFV deal is ~15% below the awarded price (a standard procurement-discipline benchmark), the **competitive-process foregone** on a €3.34 bn IFV deal is **~€500 m**.
- The OPV programme at €350 m/vessel vs Bulgaria's €251.5 m/vessel implies a **~40% premium**, of which a portion (perhaps half) reflects legitimate Mangalia-shipyard revival cost and the rest a competitive-process discount foregone. Excess cost: **~€200 m**.
- Across the portfolio, plausible competitive-process loss: **€700 m – €1.5 bn NPV**.

#### 5.2.4 Administrative / absorption-capacity costs

- The opportunity cost of MIPE / MApN / MFP bandwidth diverted to SAFE is **non-zero but small in money terms** (~€50–100 m of staff and consultant cost).
- The bigger risk is **cohesion-fund absorption suffering** — if SAFE administrative load reduces 2024–2027 cohesion absorption by 5 percentage points, that's **€1.5 bn of lost grants** (Romania's cohesion absorption is at 17% mid-2025).
- Expected cost (probability-weighted): **€500 m – €1 bn**.

#### 5.2.5 Net NPV

| Component | Range (€ m PV) |
|---|---|
| Financial saving from cheaper borrowing | +5,000 to +8,000 |
| Capability acceleration value | +250 to +750 |
| Competitive-process loss / lock-in | −700 to −1,500 |
| Admin / absorption-capacity cost | −500 to −1,000 |
| **Net NPV** | **+3,050 to +6,250** |

**Conclusion:** Even on a conservative read, SAFE delivers a **positive NPV of €3–6 bn** for Romania vs the no-SAFE counterfactual. The financial saving from cheaper borrowing is the dominant positive component; the supplier-lock-in and absorption-capacity costs are the dominant negative components but do not flip the sign.

The result is *less* positive than the headline narrative of "free EU money for defence", because:
- A significant share of the procurement would have happened anyway.
- The lock-in costs are real and material.
- SAFE debt is still debt — it lifts the debt-to-GDP ratio by ~4 pp at a time when Romania is one ratings notch above junk.

The result is *more* positive than the strongest critique ("German bailout funded by Romanian taxpayers"), because:
- The interest saving is genuine and large in absolute terms (~€500 m/yr during grace).
- The Rheinmetall share, while high, comes with real Romanian-soil investment (Automecanica Mediaș, Victoria JV, Mangalia administration) that creates jobs and capability that would not have existed otherwise.

### 5.2.6 Sanity check — interest savings over the grace period only

A simpler, more transparent way to see the saving: in the **10-year grace period (2026–2035)**, Romania pays only interest. At 3% the annual cost is **€500 m**; at a market 6% it would be **€1 bn**. The cumulative interest cost saving across the 10-year grace period alone is therefore:

| Counterfactual rate | Cumulative grace-period saving |
|---|---|
| 5.5% (RO EUR low) | (€917m − €500m) × 10 ≈ **€4.2 bn** |
| 6.0% (RO EUR mid) | (€1,001m − €500m) × 10 ≈ **€5.0 bn** |
| 6.5% (RO EUR high) | (€1,084m − €500m) × 10 ≈ **€5.8 bn** |

This is **nominal cash saving, undiscounted, over the next 10 years** — a horizon a Romanian Minister of Finance can defend with a straight face. Discounted at 5%, the PV of just-the-grace-period saving is roughly **€3.5–4.5 bn**. That is the "headline subsidy" that political leaders are referring to when they say SAFE is half-priced money.

### 5.3 Key sensitivities

The NPV calculation is most sensitive to:
1. **Romania's market borrowing rate (5.5–6.5% range)** — every 50 bp moves the financial-saving PV by ~€1 bn.
2. **Whether the 3% SAFE rate holds** — if SAFE turns out to be 3.5–4% effective, the saving shrinks by ~25–40%.
3. **Probability of a Romanian sovereign downgrade** — a downgrade would *increase* the value of the SAFE-fixed-rate locked-in pricing (because Romanian sovereign EUR yields would blow out by ~100–150 bp).
4. **PNRR / cohesion absorption rate** — if SAFE crowds out €1.5–2 bn of cohesion grants, the NPV could move to slightly positive or even neutral.

### 5.3b The PNRR-SAFE-cohesion three-way interaction

A specific concern that emerges from Section 3.2 — administrative congestion — deserves explicit treatment. As of May 2026 Romania is simultaneously managing:

1. **PNRR**: €13.6 bn grants + €7.8 bn loans, milestone-based, hard deadline **31 August 2026** for completion (with Commission payments to **31 December 2026**).
2. **Cohesion Policy 2021–2027**: ~€31 bn envelope, ~17% absorbed by mid-2025, multi-year deadlines through 2029.
3. **SAFE**: €16.68 bn loan, milestone-based, single-state procurement deadline **31 May 2026**, full availability to **31 December 2030**.
4. **CEF (Connecting Europe Facility), Modernisation Fund, JTF (Just Transition Fund)**: smaller envelopes, multi-year.

The MIPE (Min. of Investment and European Projects) and the line ministries are working at the limits of administrative capacity. **The most likely failure mode is not that SAFE goes unused**; it is that **PNRR or cohesion absorption suffers because SAFE administrative load displaces it**.

The hierarchy of pressure:
- **PNRR has the earliest hard deadline** (Aug 2026 + Dec 2026 payments) and the harshest penalty (lose-it-or-use-it).
- **SAFE has the next earliest hard deadline** (May 2026 contract-signing, then milestones through 2030) and politically high visibility.
- **Cohesion has the longest tail** and is the most easily de-prioritised.

The empirical evidence — €6.3 bn of PNRR projects at risk of missing the 2026 deadline; cohesion absorption at 17% in mid-2025 — suggests that the system is already overloaded. Adding SAFE on top will, on the margin, likely **further depress cohesion absorption** rather than be displaced itself (because SAFE's political profile is too high to neglect).

Net impact on the NPV: if SAFE-induced administrative displacement costs €1.5–2 bn of foregone cohesion grants over 2026–2029, this is a real economic cost that should be charged to the SAFE balance sheet. The §5.2.4 estimate (€500 m – €1 bn) is plausibly **understated**; a more honest estimate might be **€1–2 bn PV**.

That said, even at the high end of this estimate, the net NPV of SAFE for Romania remains positive (€2–5 bn rather than €3–6 bn).

### 5.4 What this means for the H5 hypothesis

H5 (Net positive for Romania): "Even accounting for German share, SAFE delivers net positive value to Romania via capability acquisition + infrastructure (A7/A8) + industrial participation, vs. the counterfactual of self-financing the same at market rates."

**Verdict (this task's contribution):** **Inconclusive leaning confirmed.** The macro-fiscal layer says yes, NPV is positive in the €3–6 bn range. But the verdict depends on Tasks 3, 4, and 6:
- If the Lynx selection turns out to be a competitive-process foregone of >€1 bn (Task 3 will tell), the NPV margin shrinks but stays positive.
- If Romanian industrial participation falls below 40% (Tasks 2, 6), the NPV margin shrinks further.
- If absorption capacity issues cost more than €1.5 bn in lost cohesion funds (Task 6), the NPV could turn marginal.

The financial saving from cheaper borrowing is the floor that prevents the NPV from going negative under any plausible scenario short of a major scandal or a hard sovereign downgrade.

### 5.5 Political-economy reading

The financial NPV calculation deliberately strips out political-economy effects. They matter, in both directions:

**Strengthening the case for SAFE:**
- **Treaty-style commitment**: A 45-year loan with a 10-year grace creates a durable EU-Romania connection that survives election cycles. This has option value as a deepening of the EU integration relationship — analogous to the value of the original PNRR commitment.
- **Insurance value**: Romania is a front-line Black Sea / Moldova / Ukraine-border state. The capability acceleration value is **higher than the simple 5–15% acceleration premium** if the deterrence horizon is taken seriously. A 2027 conflict scenario in the region would make 12 months of acceleration on air defence and IFVs worth multiples of the nominal premium.
- **Industrial-policy value**: Even if the Rheinmetall share is high, the **Automecanica Mediaș + Victoria + Mangalia** investments are 10–25-year footprints in Romanian industrial geography. Once Rheinmetall has invested €500 m+ in Romania, the political-economy logic of *further* Rheinmetall investment grows (a path-dependence effect that benefits Romania regardless of the headline contractor split).

**Weakening the case for SAFE:**
- **Lock-in to sub-optimal procurement**: The May 2026 contract-signing deadline forced decisions on the Lynx IFV that probably do not reflect best-value-for-money. If the next 5–10 years of MApN procurement is path-dependent on those choices (logistics, training, doctrine), the lock-in cost compounds.
- **Sovereignty cost**: Conditionality is real. A future Romanian government — particularly one less aligned with Brussels — could find SAFE disbursement frozen as a foreign-policy lever. This is unlikely under the current Bolojan government but not impossible over a 45-year horizon.
- **Asymmetric political optics**: PSD-aligned and AUR-aligned media frame SAFE as "Germans buying Romania with German money" (technically wrong since it's EU money on-lent to Romania, but the optics stick because of the high Rheinmetall share). Each contract-signature cycle revives the political-economy fight, which itself has a cost in political capital.

### 5.6 Bottom line

The macro-fiscal arithmetic supports a **positive NPV of €3–6 bn** for Romania. The economic case for SAFE is solid. The case is *less* solid than the headline rhetoric ("free EU money for defence"), because:
- A meaningful fraction of the procurement would have happened anyway, just slower.
- Real lock-in and competitive-process costs are baked in.
- SAFE debt is still debt and lifts the debt/GDP ratio by ~4pp at a time when Romania is one notch from junk.

The case is *more* solid than the strongest critique ("German bailout funded by Romanian taxpayers"), because:
- The interest saving is large and real (€500 m/yr during grace, ~€5 bn cumulative in just the grace decade).
- The Rheinmetall share, while high, comes with substantial Romanian-soil investments (€535 m Victoria JV, Automecanica Mediaș stake, Mangalia administration) that would not have existed without SAFE timing pressure.
- The non-SAFE counterfactual is **not** "no debt"; it is "more expensive debt on top of slower procurement", which is unambiguously worse.

The verdict has to be: **SAFE is a net positive for Romania, but not unambiguously so, and the margin depends critically on the quality of the procurement decisions Romania makes by 31 May 2026.** Task 6 (transparency / contract-by-contract synthesis) is where that margin gets stress-tested.

## 6. Open questions

Items not resolved within this task's scope:

## 8. Caveats and uncertainty bracketing

- Where this document says "approximately 3% interest rate", the true effective rate could be anywhere in the **2.8–3.5% range** depending on tranche profile.
- Where this document quotes Romania's EUR sovereign yield as **5.5–6.5%**, the actual mid-yield on a hypothetical new 10y EUR issue in May 2026 might be slightly higher (closer to 6.0–6.7%) given the negative-outlook rating action.
- The NPV calculations use **5% as a discount rate**, which is a reasonable proxy for Romania's medium-term opportunity cost of capital but not a defendable single number. Changing the discount rate from 4% to 6% moves the financial-saving PV by ~€2 bn.
- The 65% / 35% origin rule applies to **end-product value**, which is operationally measured by the contracting authority (member state). There is no live Commission audit of the actual breakdown; the published share figures are essentially self-reported until 2027.
- The "Rheinmetall ~52% / €5 bn" figure for Romania is sourced to Warsight and defence-industry.eu reporting based on industry-source signals. It will be refined when Task 2's contracts ledger is fully populated. Treat as **upper-end estimate** for now.
- The "Mangalia 40% Rheinmetall stake" detail is not fully clear in primary sources — what is documented is that Rheinmetall is **administering** the shipyard following Damen's June 2024 insolvency, with majority Romanian-public ownership preserved. The 72.5% stake referenced in some reporting is for Automecanica Mediaș, not Mangalia.
- The OSW analysis quoting "anti-concentration: top-3 cannot exceed 60%" is corroborated; the rule is in the Regulation's allocation methodology. The way the formula handles late entrants and re-allocations after withdrawals is not fully documented in public sources.
