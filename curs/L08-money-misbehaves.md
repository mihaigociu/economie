## The hook: an economy born in a prison camp, out of cigarettes

In 1945, a young British economist named **R. A. Radford** published a short paper that has delighted and disturbed economists ever since: *"The Economic Organisation of a P.O.W. Camp."* He had spent the war as a prisoner, and he did what only an economist would do in a prison camp — he watched a whole monetary system *invent itself from nothing*, and took notes.

Here is what he saw. The prisoners received, irregularly, Red Cross parcels: food, chocolate, and — crucially — **cigarettes**. Not everyone smoked, everyone wanted different things, and there was no money. So, with no plan and no authority, the cigarette quietly became **money**.

And not vaguely. Fully. Watch how far it went:

- **Prices** emerged and stabilised: a tin of jam might go for a set number of cigarettes, a shirt for a larger number. A shop opened; prices were even *posted on a bulletin board*.
- **A unit of account** appeared: men reckoned value "in cigarettes" the way we reckon in euros, even for things they'd never buy.
- **A store of value**: you could hold your wealth as a stash of cigarettes and spend it later.
- **Inflation and deflation** arrived, obeying the quantity of money. When a big cigarette delivery landed, the camp was flush — prices of everything *rose* (more money chasing the same goods). Between deliveries, prisoners literally **smoked the currency**; the money supply shrank, and prices *fell*.
- Even a crude **credit market** and a kind of **central bank** appeared — a camp shop that issued paper receipts backed by a store of food, stabilising the "cigarette price level."

A complete monetary economy, with its own boom, bust, and monetary policy — grown spontaneously from a Red Cross parcel. Radford's camp is the single best laboratory we have for the question this lesson answers: *what is money, really — and why does it misbehave so badly that it becomes the source of half of capitalism's diseases?*

## The textbook's three functions — and the politics they hide

Open any textbook and money is defined by three jobs, all of which the cigarettes performed:

1. **Medium of exchange** — the thing you swap for goods (cigarettes for jam).
2. **Unit of account** — the yardstick you measure value in (prices "in cigarettes").
3. **Store of value** — a way to carry wealth across time (a stash for later).

This is true as far as it goes. But notice what the tidy list does: it presents money as a *neutral tool* — a convenient technology, like a ruler or a wheelbarrow, with no politics in it. That framing is not innocent. It is the beginning of the great story we must now interrogate — the story that money is a **veil**.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Wait — doesn't the cigarette story prove the free-market view? Money just* appeared*, naturally, with no government, no politics. Isn't that exactly 'money is a neutral, natural tool'?"*
>
> Sharp — and it's the objection the story is *designed* to provoke, so let's meet it head-on. Yes, money can emerge spontaneously from convention; the camp proves it. But look closer and the "natural, apolitical" reading falls apart in three ways. First, even in the camp money was *social and political*: the shop-with-receipts was a tiny government, and disputes over prices, hoarding, and "counterfeit" (wetting cigarettes to fatten them!) were disputes about *power and fairness*, not just convenience. Second — and this is the giant point — the camp's money was a *real commodity in fixed supply* (you can't print cigarettes). **Our** money isn't. Ours is created by banks and the state, out of nothing, as debt (Lesson 4, and next lesson) — which makes it vastly more powerful, more political, and more unstable than any cigarette. Third, the camp itself shows money is *not* the innocent veil the textbook implies — because it produced booms and busts *on its own*. Hold that thought; it's the heart of the lesson.

## Is money a "veil"? The most important question in the subject

For two centuries, mainstream ("classical") economists have told a reassuring story called the **neutrality of money**. It goes:

> Money is just a **veil** draped over the *real* economy. Underneath, what matters is real stuff — goods, labour, machines, and the barter-like exchange of them. Money merely lubricates those real exchanges. Double the money and you just double all prices; the *real* economy (how much is produced, how many are employed) is unchanged. Money is a tool, not a force. It has no life of its own.

If that were true, money would be boring — a technicality for accountants. **Varoufakis, following Keynes, insists it is false, and that the falseness is the key to understanding crises.** Money is *not* a neutral veil. It is an active force that reaches into the real economy and moves it — changing not just prices but *output and employment*. Here is why, and it comes straight back to the cigarettes.

The troublemaker is function 3: **store of value.** Think about what it lets you do. In a pure barter world, if you sell your wheat you *must* immediately want something back — there's nowhere to "park" the value. But money lets you sell *now* and buy *later* — or *never*. It lets you **hold** your purchasing power instead of spending it. And that single power — to withhold — is the crack through which crisis enters.

## Hoarding: how money lets demand disappear 🕳️

Follow the logic slowly, because it's one of the most important chains in the whole course.

- Every euro someone *doesn't* spend is a sale someone else *doesn't* make. Your spending is my income (remember Lesson 7!). So if enough people, frightened about the future, decide to **hoard money** rather than spend it, total demand across the economy falls.
- Firms see their goods going unsold. They cut production and lay off workers. Those workers now have less income, so *they* spend less too — and the spiral feeds itself.
- Here's the sting: **everyone is behaving sensibly.** In frightening times, holding cash is prudent for each individual. But when *all* individuals do it at once, they collectively destroy the demand that would have kept them employed. What is rational for one is ruinous for all — the **fallacy of composition** again, which we'll meet as the *paradox of thrift* in Lesson 20.

This is why money is not neutral. In a barter economy you *cannot* have a "general glut" — a situation where *everything* is unsold at once — because every sale is instantly a purchase. But money, by letting people **hold demand back**, makes a general collapse of spending possible. **Money is the thing that lets an economy seize up.** The camp foreshadowed it: between cigarette deliveries, as men hoarded and smoked their money, trade withered. Scale that up, add banks, and you have 1929 and 2008.

> 🎭 So the "veil" is exactly backwards. Money is not a passive film over a real economy that would hum along fine without it. Money is what makes the real economy capable of *stalling* — of leaving workers idle and goods unsold not because anything real is missing, but because the thing that links buying and selling across time can be *withheld*. Keynes's revolution, in one sentence: **unemployment is often a monetary phenomenon — a shortage not of stuff, but of spending.**

## The "price of money" that won't clear either

Recall Lesson 5's promise: money is a **fictitious, misbehaving commodity**, and its "price" is the **interest rate** — the cost of borrowing money, or the reward for lending it. The mainstream hopes interest behaves like the price of apples: if there's too little investment, the interest rate falls until borrowing becomes attractive and the economy self-corrects. Tidy.

But it doesn't reliably work — for the same reason wages don't clear (Lesson 7). In a slump, people and firms are so frightened that they want to hold cash *no matter how low interest rates go*. You can cut the interest rate to zero — you can practically *pay* people to borrow — and still they won't, because in terror the safest asset is money itself. Keynes called this the **liquidity trap**: the price of money hits the floor and *still* fails to clear the market. The self-correction the textbook promises simply stops working, exactly when it's needed most. (This is why, in 2008 and after, near-zero rates still couldn't restart the economy on their own — Part IV.)

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Okay, but why does it* matter *so much whether money is 'neutral'? It feels like a technical squabble between professors."*
>
> Because everything downstream hangs on it — it is the most consequential fork in all of economics. **If money is a neutral veil**, then slumps must be caused by something *real* (workers demanding too-high wages, governments meddling), the cure is to cut wages and let markets clear, and mass unemployment is basically the workers' own fault. **If money is not neutral** — if slumps are caused by a collapse of *spending* that money makes possible — then cutting wages makes it *worse* (less income, less demand, Lesson 7), and the cure is to *restore spending* (by government, by central banks). The entire austerity-vs-stimulus war of Part V, the whole fight over the 2008 response, every argument about whether cutting public spending in a recession is wisdom or madness — all of it is downstream of this one "technical squabble." Which is why the professors fight about it so bitterly. It's never been technical.

## A last gift from the camp: Gresham's Law

One more thing Radford noticed, because it's a lovely, memorable law you'll now spot everywhere. The camp had two grades of cigarette: fine hand-rolled ones and rougher machine-made ones, both counting as "money." What happened? Prisoners *spent* the bad (machine-made) cigarettes and *hoarded or smoked* the good ones. The good money vanished from circulation; only the bad kept changing hands.

This is **Gresham's Law**: *"bad money drives out good."* When two monies circulate at the same official value but one is intrinsically better, people rationally pass on the inferior one and keep the superior — so the good money disappears from trade. It's a perfect little illustration of the store-of-value tension at the heart of money: the better a thing is at *holding* value, the more people *withhold* it from circulation — the same withholding instinct that, scaled up, becomes hoarding and crisis.

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

| Term | In one honest sentence |
|------|------------------------|
| **The three functions of money** | Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value — true, but framed to make money look like a neutral, apolitical tool. |
| **Neutrality of money / the "veil"** | The classical claim that money merely lubricates a "real" barter-like economy and changing its quantity changes only prices — which Keynes and Varoufakis reject. |
| **Store of value (the troublemaker)** | Money's power to be *held* rather than spent — the crack through which crisis enters, because withheld money is withheld demand. |
| **Hoarding & the general glut** | When many hold money instead of spending, total demand collapses and goods go unsold everywhere — impossible under barter, made possible by money. |
| **Liquidity trap** | A slump so fearful that even a near-zero interest rate can't coax people to borrow and spend — the "price of money" fails to clear. |
| **Gresham's Law** | "Bad money drives out good": when two monies share an official value, people spend the inferior and hoard the superior. |

## The takeaway

A whole monetary economy — prices, inflation, deflation, credit, even a central bank — grew spontaneously from cigarettes in a prison camp, which tells us money is a **social convention**, not a natural substance. But the camp also exposes the lie in the textbook's tidy picture. Money is *not* a neutral **veil** over a "real" economy that would hum along without it. Its third function — **store of value** — gives money the power to be *withheld*, and withheld money is **withheld demand**. That is the crack through which crisis enters: when frightened people all hoard at once, spending collapses, goods go unsold, and workers are laid off — not because anything *real* is missing, but because money let everyone hold back at the same time. And the "price of money," the interest rate, can't reliably fix it — in a **liquidity trap** it hits zero and still fails to clear. So money is the **second fictitious, misbehaving commodity** (labour was the first), and its misbehaviour — hoarding, gluts, liquidity traps — is why capitalism suffers slumps that no barter economy ever could. Whether you believe this or believe the "veil" decides which side you take in every crisis argument for the rest of your life.

In a recession the government spending *more* (not less) can be what stops the spiral — the opposite of the "thrift is always a virtue" intuition.
