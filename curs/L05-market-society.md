## The hook: when sheep ate the people

In 1516, Thomas More looked at what was happening to the English countryside and wrote a sentence that still stops me cold. The sheep of England, he said, once so gentle,

> *"have become so great devourers, and so wild, that they eat up and swallow down the very men themselves."*

Sheep eating men? He wasn't mad. He was describing an economic revolution in a metaphor sharper than any diagram. Wool had become hugely profitable on the export market. So landlords looked at the **common land** — the fields, pastures, and woods that ordinary peasants had farmed and grazed together for centuries under customary right — and did the arithmetic: *sheep are worth more than tenants.* They fenced the commons off, evicted the villagers, and filled the land with sheep. The people who had lived there for generations were simply... removed. The sheep had, quite literally, swallowed them down.

This process — repeated over three centuries across England and beyond — has a dry name: **the enclosures.** And I want to convince you it is one of the most important events in the history of the human race, because it is the moment a *society with markets* was violently converted into a **market society**. It is where "the economy" as you and I experience it was *born* — not discovered, not evolved gently, but *made*, by specific people, through acts of force.

Last two lessons gave us surplus (L2) and money-as-debt (L4). Today we witness the third and final great leap of Part I: the leap that turned human beings themselves into something bought and sold.

## The distinction, now with its origin story

Back in Lesson 1 I drew a line you were told to read twice:

- a **society with markets** — old, near-universal; the marketplace is *one tool among many*, embedded in custom, family, obligation;
- a **market society** — recent, made; *almost everything* is bought and sold, including things that had never before been ordinary merchandise.

In Lesson 1 that was just a distinction. Now I owe you the *mechanism* — how the first became the second — and the enclosures are the answer. Here's the logic, step by step:

1. For most of history, peasants had **access to land** — the commons — which meant access to *the means of producing their own survival*. They grew their own food. They were poor, often oppressed, but they did **not** have to sell themselves to anyone to eat.
2. The enclosures **cut that link**. Fence off the commons, and the peasant loses access to the means of survival. Now she owns nothing that can feed her — nothing except one thing: **her own capacity to work.**
3. To eat, she must now **sell that capacity** — rent out her hours — to whoever *does* own the means of production (the land, the workshop, later the factory). She must become a **wage labourer.**

And there it is. A whole class of people has been *manufactured* who own nothing to sell but their own labour, and who must therefore enter the market not as an occasional convenience but as a matter of *life and death, every single day.* That is the market society. Not "markets exist" — markets always existed — but "**you must sell yourself on the market to live.**"

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"But working for a wage is just… normal. People have always worked. Why are you making it sound sinister?"*
>
> Working is ancient — of course. What's historically *new and specific* is **wage labour**: selling your labour-time as a commodity, by the hour, to someone who owns the tools and keeps what you produce. A medieval serf worked, hard, but he wasn't a wage labourer — he was bound to land he had customary rights to, and he kept or owed a share of his *own* crop. A self-sufficient peasant worked, but sold no labour. The idea that the normal way to get bread is to *rent yourself out* is only a few centuries old. It feels eternal precisely *because* it won so completely — and Lesson 1 warned you: what feels like eternal human nature is often a recent construction wearing a disguise. That disguise is the thing I most want you to be able to see through.

## The three things that were never made to be sold: fictitious commodities ⚖️

The economist-historian **Karl Polanyi**, in his 1944 masterpiece *The Great Transformation*, gave the sharpest name to what the market society did. He said it turned three things into **"fictitious commodities."**

A genuine commodity is something *produced in order to be sold* — a chair, a loaf, a phone. But the market society dragged onto the auction block three things that were **never produced for sale at all**:

- **Labour** — this is just *human beings living their lives*; "labour" is not manufactured to be sold, it's a person's time and energy and dignity.
- **Land** — this is just *nature*; it was here before us, not produced by anyone for a market.
- **Money** — this is, as we saw in Lesson 4, just *circulating trust/debt*, a token of social relations, not a thing produced to be traded.

Polanyi's point is devastating: pretending these three are ordinary commodities is a **useful fiction that is also profoundly dangerous**, because they don't *behave* like chairs and loaves. And two of them — the two Varoufakis fixes on — misbehave so badly that they generate capitalism's worst diseases:

- When the price of **labour** (the wage) is left to a "free" market, it does *not* smoothly clear like the price of apples. Instead you get **mass unemployment** — millions unable to sell their labour at any wage the market offers, i.e. unable to live. (We open this in Lesson 7.)
- When the price of **money** (the interest rate) is left to a "free" market, it does *not* smoothly clear either. Instead you get **booms, bubbles, and financial crashes** — credit surging and collapsing. (Lessons 8–10.)

This is the thread from Lesson 1 you were told to keep in your pocket — *"the two great commodities that misbehave."* Now you know their names (labour and money), where they came from (the market society, born in the enclosures), and why they're trouble (they're fictions — real human lives and real social trust, dressed up as merchandise). **Half of macroeconomics is the study of these two fictions breaking down.**

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Still — wasn't the end of feudalism a good thing? The peasants were freed from serfdom, weren't they?"*
>
> Yes, and this is the heart of it, so hold both halves at once. The dispossessed peasant became **"doubly free,"** in a bitterly double sense. Free *from* serfdom — no lord can command her, she can move, choose an employer, sign a contract as a legal equal: a genuine and hard-won liberation. **And** free *from* the land — that is, stripped of it, owning nothing, "free" in the way a person is "free" of a coat in winter. The market society hands you real freedom with one hand and takes away the material independence that would let you *use* it with the other. Whenever someone celebrates the "free market" and the "free labourer," ask which of the two freedoms they mean — the freedom *to*, or the freedom *from having anything.* Usually they're quietly counting on you to hear only the first.

## The double movement: society fights back ↔️

One more idea from Polanyi, because it will save you from a lazy conclusion. You might think: *so the market society steamrolls everything and that's the end of the story.* It isn't — and Polanyi named why.

Because treating human beings and nature as pure commodities is so destructive — it grinds up workers, wrecks communities, exhausts the land — **society spontaneously pushes back.** Whenever the market tries to fully commodify labour, land, and money, a counter-movement rises to protect society from being torn apart: trade unions, factory acts, the ten-hour day, public health, pensions, unemployment insurance, environmental law, central banks tasked with steadying money. Polanyi called this the **double movement**: a pendulum swinging between the drive to marketise *everything* and society's self-defence against being consumed.

This matters enormously for how you'll read the news for the rest of your life. Almost every big economic-political fight — over labour law, welfare, financial regulation, climate policy — is a round in this same 200-year contest between the market's drive to commodify and society's drive to protect the un-commodifiable. Once you can see the double movement, you can locate any headline on the pendulum's arc.

## The takeaway

Markets are ancient and mostly benign; the **market society** is recent and was *made* — born in England when the enclosures fenced off the commons, cut ordinary people off from the means of their own survival, and left them with nothing to sell but themselves. That single act of dispossession manufactured **wage labour** and, with it, the modern "economy." Polanyi named the deep trick: the market society turns **labour, land, and money** into **fictitious commodities** — things never made to be sold — and two of them, labour and money, misbehave so badly (mass unemployment; financial crashes) that they generate capitalism's chronic crises. But society fights back: the **double movement** is the 200-year pendulum between commodifying everything and protecting society from it — the hidden shape of nearly every economic-political battle you'll ever read about. Part I is complete: we have all the parts of the machine. Part II turns it on.

<summary><strong>▶ Answer key & explanations (open after answering)</strong></summary>

**Q1 — (b).** *Concept: the enclosures.* Wool's profitability led landlords to enclose common land for sheep and evict the peasants — More's metaphor for people being displaced by a more profitable use of land. The market society is being born.

**Q2.** *Concept: manufacturing wage labour.* (1) Peasants had **access to the commons** — i.e. access to the means of producing their own survival, so they did not need to sell themselves to eat. (2) The enclosures **fenced off the commons and evicted them**, cutting that link and leaving them owning nothing that can feed them — *except their own capacity to work.* (3) To survive they must now **sell that labour for wages** to whoever owns the means of production — becoming wage labourers. *Full credit traces access-to-means → dispossession → forced sale of labour.*

**Q3.** *Concept: fictitious commodities.* **Labour, land, and money.** They are "fictitious" because none of them was *produced in order to be sold* (labour is human life/time; land is nature; money is circulating trust/debt) — yet the market society treats them as ordinary commodities, which is a useful but dangerous fiction because they don't behave like produced goods. *Full credit: all three named + the "never produced for sale" criterion.*

**Q4 — False (or "only half-true").** *Concept: doubly free.* The dispossessed worker became free *from* serfdom (a real liberation — legal equality, mobility, choice of employer) **and** free *from* the land (stripped of the material independence that let peasants feed themselves). So it was liberation and dispossession at once; calling it "straightforwardly good" hears only the first freedom and ignores the second. *Full credit requires naming both freedoms.*

**Q5.** *Concept: labour as a misbehaving fictitious commodity + the double movement.* A strong objection makes these moves:
- **Labour is not apples.** It's a *fictitious commodity* (Polanyi) — real human lives, not something produced for sale. Pricing it like apples is a category error.
- **It misbehaves.** Unlike the apple market, a "free" labour market does *not* smoothly clear: it produces **mass unemployment** — people unable to sell their labour at any offered wage, i.e. unable to live — because the wage is not just another price but the condition of survival, and because demand for labour depends on aggregate spending (foreshadowing Part V's fallacy of composition).
- **The protections are the double movement.** Minimum wage, unions, and unemployment insurance are exactly society's self-defence against the destructiveness of fully commodifying labour. Abolishing them doesn't "free" the market; it removes the guardrails the double movement erected precisely because unprotected labour markets grind people up.
- **Cui prodest? (L1 toolkit)** "Truly free market" names winners (employers gaining bargaining power over desperate workers) while dressing a power shift as a natural law. *Full credit: at least the fictitious-commodity point plus one of misbehaviour / double movement, applied concretely.*
