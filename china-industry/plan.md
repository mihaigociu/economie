# China's Industrial Rise — Research Plan

## Thesis (to be tested, not assumed)

China's industry has progressed through three overlapping phases:

1. **Absorb & scale** (1978–~2005): special economic zones, technology transfer requirements, foreign direct investment, low-cost manufacturing — the "world's factory" era.
2. **Catch up & substitute** (~2005–~2015): heavy R&D ramp-up, indigenous innovation policies, fast-follower products, import substitution in strategic sectors.
3. **Lead at the frontier — selectively** (~2015–present): in a small but consequential set of sectors — **lithium-ion batteries, electric vehicles, robotics (industrial robots, drones, humanoids), and parts of applied AI** — Chinese firms now set the global pace on cost, scale, and in some dimensions on technology itself, while continuing to import or imitate in others (advanced semiconductors, commercial aviation, branded biopharma, several defense sub-domains).

Layered onto this is a structural fact: through three decades of state-guided investment, China has come to **dominate the upstream and midstream of several critical supply chains** (rare-earth refining, battery cells and precursor chemicals, solar PV, key pharmaceutical intermediates). That dominance is now a foreign-policy instrument as much as an industrial one.

A third structural advantage — increasingly visible and arguably the most consequential for the next decade — is **energy**. China's electricity generation already exceeds the US and EU combined; its renewable, nuclear, hydro and grid build-outs operate at a scale and speed Western systems cannot currently match. In an era where AI training and inference are becoming **energy-bound** rather than purely chip-bound, and where the green industrial transition (EVs, electrolysis, heat pumps, data centres) is electricity-intensive, this matters. China is on track to be the only major economy where the answer to "can we power the next 100 GW of new industrial and AI load?" is unambiguously yes.

The brief's purpose is to test how far the third claim ("selective frontier leadership"), the supply-chain leverage claim, and the energy-asset claim actually hold up against public data — where each is strongest and weakest, and what that means for Western policy.

The analysis is **narrative-first but evidence-led** in the same style as `it-romania/`: every claim is anchored to a public dataset or freely-available report; charts are generated by Python scripts from raw CSVs committed to `raw-data/`.

Scope: roughly **2000–present**, with key qualitative anchors going back to 1978. Quantitative comparisons centre on the last ~15 years where international datasets are densest.

### Methodology Note — Chinese Official Statistics

Chinese NBS data has known reliability concerns (GDP smoothing, provincial inflation of figures, periodic revisions). We treat it as follows:
- **For trade**: prefer mirror data — China's exports cross-checked against partner-country import data via UN Comtrade.
- **For R&D and patents**: NBS R&D-spending series is broadly aligned with OECD's reconstruction of Chinese R&D; we use OECD where available, NBS as the underlying source.
- **For sectoral output (autos, batteries, etc.)**: industry associations (CAAM, CPCA) are tighter than NBS aggregates and align with importer-country customs data.
- **For GDP and macro**: we use World Bank and IMF reconstructions, noting the caveat.

Every chart specifies its source row in the underlying CSV file, and we **never use a Chinese number we cannot independently corroborate** for a load-bearing claim.

### 7. Sectoral Deep Dive — Robotics & Industrial Automation
**Goal:** the fourth frontier-leadership case. China is by far the world's largest market for industrial robots, is closing fast on Japanese/German makers on technology, dominates consumer drones, and is now leading the commercial roll-out of humanoid robotics. Robotics sits at the intersection of manufacturing scale, the EV/battery supply chain, and applied AI — which makes it the natural successor frontier to all three earlier deep-dives.

#### 7.1 The Market — Annual Installations
- IFR *World Robotics* report (annual): industrial-robot installations by country, 2010–latest
- China's share of new global installations rose from ~15% in 2010 to ~50%+ by 2023 — more than the US, EU27, Japan, and Korea combined in a single year
- Robot density (industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers): China overtook the US in 2021 and is closing on Germany; Korea remains the world leader

#### 7.2 Domestic Producers Rising
- The original "Big Four" of industrial robotics: FANUC and Yaskawa (Japan), ABB (Switzerland/Sweden), KUKA (Germany → acquired by Midea 2016)
- Chinese challengers gaining domestic-market share: Estun, Inovance, Siasun, Han's Robot, EFORT — domestic share rose from ~30% in 2015 toward ~50% by 2024 (IFR + Chinese MIIT data + JRC reports)
- The KUKA acquisition (2016) as a milestone in the technology-flow story — primary-source filings only

#### 7.3 Humanoid Robotics — The 2024–2025 Inflection
- Multiple Chinese humanoid-robot makers reached commercial pilot stage in 2024–25: Unitree, Fourier Intelligence, UBTECH, AGIBOT, Xiaomi, BYD-backed entrants
- Pricing: Unitree's G1 humanoid retailed below $20k by late 2024 — an order of magnitude under Western equivalents
- Why China is structurally well-positioned: shared supply chain with EVs and batteries (motors, actuators, lithium cells, power electronics), abundant low-cost precision-manufacturing labour, explicit policy support via the "new productive forces" catalog
- Honest caveats: most current humanoids are demonstrators rather than deployed-at-scale products; total deployed units globally remain small; productisation timelines are uncertain and the inflection could disappoint

#### 7.4 Drones (UAVs)
- DJI's dominance of the consumer / prosumer drone market — ~70%+ global share by units (public DJI disclosures + US federal procurement data)
- Industrial drones: XAG (agriculture), EHang (passenger / eVTOL, NASDAQ-listed), AutoFlight, Meituan (logistics)
- The US Blue UAS list and DoD restrictions on Chinese drones — a case study in technology dependence and policy response
- Note: military-export UCAVs (CASC Rainbow, AVIC Wing Loong) overlap with §9.5 below

#### 7.5 Automation Inside Chinese Manufacturing
- "Lights-out" factory adoption — EV factories (BYD, NIO, Xpeng), battery plants (CATL, BYD), white-goods (Midea, Haier) — primary-source filings and disclosures only
- AI-driven visual QC deployment in electronics assembly
- The labour-cost-crossover argument: Chinese manufacturing wages rose 4–5x in real terms over 2005–2020 (NBS, China Labour Bulletin), and automation became cheaper than additional hiring at the margin

#### 7.6 The Tech Gap That Remains
- **Precision reducers** (harmonic drives, RV reducers) — Japanese makers (Harmonic Drive Systems, Nabtesco) still dominate the high end; Chinese substitutes (Leaderdrive, Laifual) closing but not at parity for the most demanding applications
- **High-end servo motors and controllers** — Japanese/German lead in highest-precision applications
- **Industrial-robot software and digital twins** — Western dominance (Siemens, Rockwell, Dassault) — overlaps with §9.4

#### 7.7 Implications
- Robotics is the convergence point of three previously-separate Chinese strengths: manufacturing scale, the EV/battery components supply chain, and applied AI from §6
- If humanoid robotics commercialises at scale, it is plausibly the most consequential new product category of the decade — and China starts with structural advantages no other country currently matches
- Counter-case: humanoid roll-out timelines could disappoint, in which case industrial-robot leadership remains the durable story

**Scripts:** `06_robotics.py` — IFR *World Robotics* CSVs, UN Comtrade HS 8479.50 (industrial robots) and 8525.89 (drones), Chinese MIIT industrial-robot production statistics, NBS manufacturing wage series, public IPO/listing filings for major Chinese robotics firms. ~8 charts.

### 8. Energy — The AI-Era Strategic Asset
**Goal:** establish that China's energy build-out is itself a strategic asset, particularly in the AI era. The argument: AI looks chip-bound today, but the deeper constraint is **energy** — and on that axis, China is uniquely positioned.

#### 8.1 Scale of the Chinese Power System
- Total electricity generation, 1990–latest, China vs US vs EU27 (Ember Global Electricity Review)
- China's annual generation now exceeds the US and EU27 combined, and the gap is widening
- Installed capacity by source 2000–latest (Ember + IRENA + CEC)
- New capacity additions per year — China adds more capacity in a single year than most large countries have in total

#### 8.2 The Generation Mix
- Coal still ~55% of generation but its share is falling; thermal-coal capacity factor declining as the role shifts toward flexibility / back-up
- Solar PV: in 2023–2024 China installed more solar capacity than the rest of the world combined (IRENA, IEA)
- Wind: same pattern; China is now the largest offshore-wind market
- Hydro: Three Gorges + continued downstream build-out — already the world's largest hydro generator
- Nuclear: ~55 reactors operating, ~30 under construction — the world's largest nuclear build pipeline by a wide margin (IAEA PRIS)
- Honest acknowledgement of the "still building coal" critique: new coal continues to be approved, even as renewable additions outrun it in capacity terms

#### 8.3 The Grid — The Underrated Asset
- Ultra-High-Voltage DC transmission (UHV-DC): China operates more UHV-DC lines than the rest of the world combined (CEC, JRC)
- Western counterpart problem: US grid interconnection queues run 5–7 years; new long-distance transmission lines take a decade-plus; NIMBY litigation is routine; EU permitting is similar
- Strategic consequence: China can co-locate GW-scale loads (AI data centres, EV factories, electrolysers, aluminium smelters) with low-cost generation and move power 2,000+ km at low loss

#### 8.4 AI's Energy Appetite — and Who Can Feed It
- IEA *Electricity 2024* and *Energy & AI 2025*: global data-centre electricity demand projected to roughly double by 2030
- US bottlenecks: Northern Virginia (PJM) at the limit, gas-turbine order books 4–5 years out, hyperscaler PPA scramble for any new generation
- EU bottlenecks: Ireland data-centre moratorium (2022), German industrial electricity-price crisis post-2022
- China: spare capacity in western provinces (Inner Mongolia, Xinjiang, Sichuan, Qinghai), low industrial power prices, ability to add new generation in years rather than a decade

#### 8.5 Industrial Electricity Prices
- Industrial electricity price by country, 2010–latest (OECD / IEA Energy Prices and Taxes)
- China's industrial rate is materially below the EU and somewhat below the US; the gap to German industry is particularly large
- Cumulative effect on manufacturing competitiveness in energy-intensive industries (aluminium, chemicals, refining, steel, and now AI compute)

#### 8.6 Policy Levers
- 14th Five-Year Plan energy chapter — dual carbon goals (peak by 2030, neutrality by 2060) operating *alongside* an explicit "energy security first" primacy after the 2021 power crisis
- Deployment mechanism: state grid + provincial competition + administrative quotas, not primarily market price signals
- Why coal expansion continues despite the rhetoric — hedge against renewable intermittency, employment in coal regions, and political economy of provincial SOEs

#### 8.7 The AI-Energy Coupling
- If frontier-model training compute continues to scale at recent rates, electricity demand for AI grows roughly in proportion
- The "chip-bound" → "energy-bound" transition: export controls bite both sides over time (Western frontier labs also race for compute), but the generation and transmission constraint binds the West *harder*
- China is on track to be the only major economy where "can we power 100 GW of new AI and industrial load by 2030?" has an unambiguous yes

#### 8.8 Honest Caveats
- China remains the world's largest annual CO₂ emitter; the renewable build-out coexists with continued coal expansion
- Renewable curtailment (generation the grid could not absorb) was a real problem; it has been improving but is not fully solved
- The industrial-electricity-price advantage has been narrowing in absolute terms even as it remains large in relative terms
- "China can power AI" does not equal "China will win AI" — chips, model architectures, talent, and capital still matter — but it removes one binding constraint the West has not yet relieved

**Scripts:** `07_energy.py` — Ember Global Electricity Review CSV, IRENA capacity database, IEA Electricity Report and *Energy & AI 2025*, IAEA PRIS for nuclear, OECD/IEA energy prices, CEC for grid and UHV-DC. ~7 charts.

### 9. Where China Is *Not* Yet Leading — The Honest Counter-Evidence
**Goal:** the credibility chapter. Pre-empts the "this is a China-puff piece" critique by showing where the imitation/dependency story is still true.

#### 9.1 Advanced Semiconductors
- China's share of fab capacity by node — strong at mature (28nm+), weak at leading-edge (7nm and below)
- SMIC vs TSMC technology gap
- EUV lithography: zero domestic capability; Dutch ASML is sole-source globally
- Chip-equipment dependency (Applied Materials, Lam, KLA, ASML, Tokyo Electron, Nikon) — chart from public SEMI data

#### 9.2 Commercial Aerospace
- COMAC C919 is operating but uses Western avionics, engines (CFM LEAP — GE/Safran), and many critical systems
- Boeing/Airbus duopoly remains unchallenged at scale; Chinese airline fleets are Western-built

#### 9.3 Branded Biopharmaceuticals
- Chinese pharma is strong in APIs (active pharmaceutical ingredients) and generics — but few originator blockbuster drugs
- Top-30 drugs by global revenue: how many are Chinese-originated? (FDA approvals data, public PhRMA / EFPIA reports)

#### 9.4 Industrial Software & Operating Systems
- CAD, EDA, PLM, ERP — Western dominance (Siemens, Dassault, Cadence, Synopsys, SAP, Oracle)
- Mobile/desktop OS — Android variants and Windows; HarmonyOS progress with honest caveats
- Cloud hyperscalers: Alibaba/Tencent/Huawei strong domestically, marginal abroad

#### 9.5 Defense Systems and Combat-Proven Force Projection
The defense picture is genuinely mixed and deserves its own treatment in this chapter.
- **Where the PLA has caught up or pulled ahead** (and which therefore *don't* belong in this "lagging" chapter — they are referenced in §3 and §7): shipbuilding tonnage (Chinese commercial + naval yards now exceed the rest of the world combined — public ONI / CRS assessments), hypersonic glide vehicles (DF-17, DF-27 reported operational ahead of US equivalents), commercial-derivative UCAVs (covered in §7.4), satellite launches per year (closing on US), conventional missile arsenals (DF series), and electronic-warfare systems
- **Where China still lags materially**:
  - **Combat jet engines**: the WS-10 has matured for the J-10/J-11 family, but the high-thrust WS-15 (for J-20 fifth-gen fighter) reached operational status only in the early 2020s — decades behind GE F119/F135 and Rolls-Royce/Pratt & Whitney equivalents. Commercial high-bypass turbofans for COMAC C919 remain entirely Western-sourced (CFM LEAP) — see §9.2.
  - **Submarine quieting**: PLAN SSNs and SSBNs are publicly assessed (US ONI testimony, CSIS open analyses) as noisier than US, UK, and French equivalents.
  - **Carrier aviation**: 3 carriers operating, none nuclear-powered; carrier-borne fixed-wing aviation experience still nascent compared with US Navy's 100+ years of carrier operations and 11 nuclear carriers.
  - **Combat-proven doctrine**: PLA has had no major external combat operation since 1979; readiness derives from exercises and training, not recent combat experience.
- **What's improving fast**: gaps close year-on-year on most measures; the durable lags are the ones requiring multi-decade tacit learning (engines, sub-quieting, carrier ops) — the same pattern seen in commercial aerospace and pharma originators.

Source rule: US Office of Naval Intelligence public testimony, CSIS open analyses, IISS *Military Balance* (free summaries), SIPRI databases. We avoid speculative satellite-imagery analysis except where it has already appeared in peer-reviewed or congressional-record sources.

#### 9.6 What's Common to the Lagging Sectors
- High accumulated tacit know-how (decades of failures), heavily IP-protected by competitors, often regulated/certified (aerospace, pharma) which slows fast-follower entry, often single-supplier choke points (EUV)

### 10. Supply-Chain Leverage
**Goal:** the second pillar of the thesis. Even where China isn't yet *innovating* at the frontier, it controls upstream chokepoints that give it leverage over those who do.

#### 10.1 Rare Earths
- Mining shares: China ~60–70% of global mine output (USGS); Myanmar/US/Australia rest
- Refining and separation: China **~85–90%** — this is the real chokepoint, not mining (IEA)
- The 2010 Senkaku embargo episode — what happened, what didn't, what the lasting effect was (referencing WTO ruling 2014)
- Recent (2023–2025) export-licensing tightenings on gallium, germanium, graphite, antimony, and (October 2024) rare-earth processing tech

#### 10.2 Battery Materials
- Lithium: mining diversified (Australia, Chile), but **refining ~70% China** (IEA)
- Cobalt: DR Congo for mining, China for refining (~75%)
- Graphite: ~90% Chinese processing
- Synthesis: cell production is the visible part; precursor chemistry is the strategic part

#### 10.3 Solar PV
- Polysilicon, wafer, cell, module: **>80% Chinese at every stage** (IEA Solar Outlook)
- The economics of why this happened (subsidies + scale + cheap power + integrated supply)
- Implications for EU and US energy-transition targets

#### 10.4 Pharma Precursors
- API dependency: a large fraction of US/EU generic antibiotics and certain APIs trace to Chinese (and Indian, partly Chinese-sourced) production
- This is harder to quantify cleanly from public data — we cite the European Commission's 2021 Strategic Dependencies report and the FDA's published API data

#### 10.5 The Leverage Question
- What does "control" actually buy? Time, not a permanent veto — but in industries with 5–10 year capacity ramp times, a 5-year leverage window is real
- How leverage has actually been deployed (2010 rare earths to Japan; 2023–2024 critical-mineral export licensing) and the limits (WTO rulings, partner-country stockpiling, alternative-supply build-outs)
- Net assessment: leverage is real, asymmetric, but **finite and decaying** as substitutes are built — the policy question is how to manage the transition window

**Scripts:** `09_supply_chains.py` — USGS, IEA Critical Minerals Outlook + Solar Outlook, UN Comtrade for specific HS codes (rare-earth oxides, polysilicon, lithium carbonate), European Commission Strategic Dependencies. ~8 charts.

### 11. How the Model Actually Works — Industrial Policy Mechanics
**Goal:** demystify the "how did they do it" — not as a recipe to copy, but as a system to understand.

#### 11.1 Guided Market, Not Command Economy
- The interaction of state direction (5-year plans, sectoral catalogues, subsidies, procurement, standards) with fierce private competition (often hundreds of firms per sector, brutal price wars, exit of the weak)
- This is qualitatively different from both Soviet planning and Western market economies; cite the work of Brandt, Naughton, Pettis for the framework (we don't reinvent the analysis)

#### 11.2 The Toolbox
- Subsidies, both direct and via state-owned bank lending
- Land grants and tax preferences via local governments competing for investment
- Public procurement preferences
- Local-content and JV requirements (legacy; mostly wound down post-WTO)
- Technology-transfer-for-market-access (legacy; called out in 2018 USTR Section 301 report)
- Standards-setting (China's push in ISO, IEC, ITU)
- Talent return: "Thousand Talents" and successor programs (USTR / NSF public assessments)

#### 11.3 What Doesn't Work
- Over-capacity cycles (steel, solar 2010s, EVs 2024–25)
- Wasted subsidies (semiconductors — large state funds with poor returns through 2018)
- Local-government debt overhang from infrastructure-led growth
- The "more debt to keep growing" problem — TSF (total social financing) trajectory

#### 11.4 What Western Industrial Policy Borrowed
- IRA (US, 2022), CHIPS Act (US, 2022), EU Net-Zero Industry Act, EU Critical Raw Materials Act, EU Chips Act
- These adapt elements (production subsidies, domestic-content requirements) — the brief notes the convergence without prescribing one model

**Scripts:** `10_policy_mechanics.py` — OECD industrial-policy data, World Bank credit-to-GDP, JRC Industrial R&D Scoreboard. ~5 charts.

### 12. What Could Derail It
**Goal:** the honest forward-looking section. Two-sided — risks to China *and* risks to "we can wait it out" Western complacency.

#### 12.1 Demographic Cliff
- Working-age population peaked ~2015 and has fallen sharply since; UN population projections
- What it means for the manufacturing labour pool and for innovation (cf. Japan post-1990)

#### 12.2 Real Estate & Local-Government Debt
- The Evergrande / Country Garden era — public IMF and BIS assessments
- Drag on consumption, drag on local-government industrial-investment capacity

#### 12.3 Capital-Allocation Distortions
- ICOR (incremental capital-output ratio) rising — more investment needed per unit of growth
- Diminishing returns from infrastructure-led growth

#### 12.4 Export Controls and Decoupling
- The cumulative effect of US chip controls 2022–2025
- "Small yard, high fence" reality: it's been getting bigger, not smaller
- Friend-shoring and the rebuild of US/EU manufacturing capacity in the targeted sectors

#### 12.5 Climate, Geopolitics, Taiwan
- The brief notes these as known unknowns; we don't try to model the tail risks but acknowledge they could dwarf everything else

#### 12.6 Counter-View — Why the "China Peaked" Narrative May Be Premature
- The same demographic argument was made for Japan in 1990 and Germany in 2005; neither stopped exporting frontier engineering products
- The export-led model continues to work for now (record trade surplus 2024)
- Frontier sectors (EVs, batteries, AI, robotics) are still expanding share, not contracting

**Scripts:** `11_headwinds.py` — UN population data, IMF and BIS public series, World Bank, OECD. ~6 charts.

### 13. Implications for Western Policy
**Goal:** the "so what" — kept tight, evidence-anchored, not prescriptive.

#### 13.1 Four Things the Data Says
1. Selective frontier leadership is real (batteries, EVs, robotics, parts of AI) — denying this leads to bad policy
2. Supply-chain leverage is real but finite — the policy window is years, not decades
3. Imitation and dependency persist in equally important sectors (advanced chips, commercial aerospace, pharma originators, large parts of industrial software, several defense sub-domains) — uniform "China leads everything" framings are also wrong
4. The energy-and-grid advantage is the underestimated cross-cutting factor — it doesn't yet decide who wins AI or industrial automation, but it changes the binding constraint, and the West has no near-term answer

#### 13.2 Policy Implications — Three Levers
- **Mitigate leverage:** strategic stockpiles, allied processing capacity, recycling — Critical Raw Materials Act benchmarks, US DPA Title III, Japan/Korea/Australia coordination
- **Compete at the frontier:** sustained R&D funding (closing GERD gap to China), production subsidies in genuinely strategic sectors, careful targeting (avoiding the over-broad-subsidy trap that hit Chinese semis pre-2018)
- **Manage interdependence:** "de-risk, don't decouple" — what that actually means in numbers (trade volumes, share of imports from China by sector, where dependency is acceptable vs strategic)

#### 13.3 Things the Brief Does Not Recommend
- Blanket decoupling (the data shows it's expensive and partial at best)
- Across-the-board tariffs (the EV tariff debate is a useful test case — narrow, sector-specific countervailing measures vs broad tariff walls)
- Underestimating China (the demographic-cliff-saves-us argument)

---

### 14. Conclusion — A New Phase, Selectively
- The thesis revisited: yes, in the four named sectors (batteries, EVs, AI, robotics), China has moved beyond imitation; no, this does not mean China leads everywhere; the supply-chain leverage layer and the energy-asset layer are separate axes that compound the picture
- What the next five years will tell us: whether the selective leadership broadens (next candidates: fusion, biotech CROs, photonics, advanced materials), whether Western counter-measures restore competitive symmetry or merely slow the gap-closing, and whether the AI–energy coupling forces a structural rebalancing of Western industrial-policy priorities

## Scope Decisions (resolved)

- **Time horizon:** 1978–present. The early decades get qualitative treatment with cited primary sources (Reform & Opening, WTO accession, MIC 2025); quantitative charts focus on 2000–latest, where international datasets are densest.
- **Comparison set:** China benchmarked against the US, EU27, Japan, and Korea — the four other innovation poles. India and Taiwan appear where directly relevant (semiconductors, pharma generics) but are not maintained as parallel comparators throughout.
- **Audience:** audience-neutral. §13 (Implications for Western Policy) treats US and EU policy responses symmetrically — IRA / CHIPS Act / Critical Raw Materials Act / Net-Zero Industry Act all get equal weight, with Japan / Korea / Australia coordination noted where relevant.
- **Tone:** analytical and neutral. §13 carries sharp policy implications but stays out of advocacy — the brief grades claims against data and refuses to be either a China-puff piece or a China-bashing piece.
- **Counter-evidence chapter (§9):** kept as a standalone chapter — credibility-critical. Folding it into the individual sector deep-dives would dilute the message that the "selective leadership" claim is precisely *selective*.
- **Depth target:** heavier than `it-romania/`. ~13 substantive chapters / ~80–100 charts. The subject merits the depth: more sub-sectors, more counter-evidence, more honest caveats per chapter.
- **Chinese NBS data:** used with the documented caveat (see Methodology Note above). Every load-bearing Chinese number is corroborated against international data (UN Comtrade mirror data, OECD R&D reconstruction, partner-country customs). Where corroboration is not possible, the claim is downgraded.
- **Robotics:** in scope as a fourth frontier-sector deep dive (§7). China's >50% share of global industrial-robot installations (IFR), DJI's drone dominance, and the 2024–25 humanoid inflection make this the natural sibling to batteries / EVs / AI.
- **Length of `report.md`:** ~60 pages of narrative + embedded chart references. The topic is heavy and the audience (policymakers) benefits from the depth.
- **Counter-evidence sectors (§9):** advanced semiconductors, commercial aerospace, branded biopharmaceuticals, industrial software & operating systems, **and defense systems** (mixed: shipbuilding tonnage and hypersonics belong elsewhere; jet engines, submarine quieting, carrier aviation, and combat-proven doctrine fit the "lagging" pattern).
