## The hook: a question your history teacher never answered

Let me start not with a definition but with a question that has bothered me since I was your age.

**Why did the British invade and colonise Australia, and not the Aboriginal Australians invade and colonise Britain?**

The lazy answer — the one that should make you angry — is that Europeans were cleverer, or worked harder, or had some racial or cultural spark the Aboriginal peoples lacked. This is not only offensive; it is *false*. The Aboriginal Australians who greeted Captain Cook were, individually, every bit as intelligent, resourceful, and inventive as the sailors on his ship. So the answer cannot live inside people's heads.

It lives in the *ground*. Eurasia happened to have geography and climate — a wide east–west band of similar seasons, and a lucky handful of grasses and animals that could be domesticated — that made large-scale agriculture possible early. Australia did not. And once you can farm at scale, something extraordinary and terrible appears, something that changes everything downstream.

That something is **surplus**: more food than the producers need to eat today.

Hold onto that word. We will spend the whole of Part I on it, because I want to convince you of a claim that sounds absurd at first: *almost everything we call "the economy" — money, debt, states, armies, writing, banks, even the idea that some people naturally give orders and others take them — grew out of the management of agricultural surplus.* The British came to Botany Bay with guns, ships, and writing not because of who they were, but because their ancestors had, by geographical accident, stumbled into surplus thousands of years earlier.

Notice what just happened. I answered a question about *history* and *geography*, and it turned out to be a question about *economics*. That is the first lesson inside the first lesson: **the economy is not a separate box labelled "money stuff." It is the water we swim in.** Which is exactly why you must not leave it to the economists.

## The red pill: economics as a story that shapes reality

You've seen *The Matrix* — or at least you know the scene. Morpheus offers Neo two pills. The blue pill: wake up believing whatever you want. The red pill: see how the world really works, however uncomfortable.

Most economics teaching hands you the blue pill. It says: *the economy is like the weather. There are natural laws — supply, demand, equilibrium — and clever people in suits discover them, the way physicists discover gravity. Your job is to memorise the laws and not ask silly questions.*

I am handing you the red pill, and here is the uncomfortable thing it reveals: **economics is not like physics, because the objects it studies can hear what economists say about them — and change their behaviour as a result.**

A stone does not fall faster because Newton predicted it would. But if a respected economist announces "the banks are about to fail," and enough people believe her, they rush to withdraw their money, and the banks *do* fail — not despite the prediction but *because* of it. The prophecy fulfils itself. (We'll meet this beast properly in Lesson 10, wearing the mask of Oedipus.)

This is why economics can never be a neutral, value-free science. The stories economists tell — "markets are efficient," "governments should balance budgets like households," "there is no alternative" — are not just descriptions. They are *interventions*. Believed widely enough, they help build the very world they claim only to observe. To study economics is therefore always, unavoidably, to take part in a fight over which story we tell.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"But that sounds like you're saying economics is just opinions, then. Anything goes?"*
>
> No — the opposite. If economics were pure natural law, your opinion wouldn't matter and you could safely leave it to the experts. Precisely *because* the stories shape reality, it matters enormously which story wins, and you have both the right and the responsibility to argue about it. "Anything goes" is the blue pill. The red pill is harder: *some stories are better than others, the evidence matters, and no expert gets to end the argument by declaring it "just science."*

## What "the economy" is — and what it is not

Let's pin down the word, because it's slippery.

When politicians say *"the economy grew 2% this year,"* they usually mean one number: **GDP**, the market value of everything bought and sold. That's useful, but notice what it quietly assumes — that the economy *is* the sum of market transactions. On that definition, the meal your grandmother cooks for the family is worth nothing (no one paid for it), but the identical meal in a restaurant is worth €30. Cut down a forest and sell the timber: GDP rises. Leave the forest standing and breathe its air: GDP records zero.

So here is our working definition, deliberately wider than the politicians':

> **The economy** is how a society organises the production of what it needs and wants, and how it *distributes* the results — who gets what, and who decides.

Two words in that sentence do all the heavy lifting: **distributes** and **decides**. Every economy, from a Stone Age village to modern Romania, has to answer three questions — *what gets produced? how? and for whom?* — and the answers are never handed down by nature. Someone, or some arrangement, decides. **Wherever there is deciding-who-gets-what, there is power.** That is why we will keep power on the table when the textbooks keep sweeping it off.

## The big distinction: a society *with* markets vs. a *market society*

Here is a difference that took humanity thousands of years to produce, and that most people never notice. Read it twice.

- A **society with markets** has existed almost everywhere, almost always. There have been marketplaces — physical or otherwise — where people swap a surplus chicken for a neighbour's surplus wheat, for as long as there has been surplus. The market is a *tool*, one activity among many, embedded in a life that also runs on custom, family, religion, and obligation.

- A **market society** is something new and strange, only a few centuries old. It is a society where *almost everything* has been turned into something bought and sold — including the two things that had never before been ordinary commodities: **human labour** (you rent out your hours to an employer) and **land/money itself**. In a market society, the market is no longer one tool among many. It becomes the master principle to which everything else — time, relationships, nature, even self-worth — must bend.

The shift from the first to the second is, in my view, the true birth of "the economy" as we now experience it. It didn't happen because it was natural or inevitable. In England it happened through the violent **enclosures** — fencing off common land that peasants had used for centuries, forcing them off it, and thereby *manufacturing* a class of people who owned nothing to sell but their own labour. (We'll return to this in Lesson 5.) Markets are ancient and often benign. The *market society* was made — by specific people, through specific acts of power — and it can, in principle, be remade.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Why does the difference matter so much to you?"*
>
> Because if you believe we've *always* lived in a market society, you'll believe it's just human nature, and that any alternative is a fantasy against the grain of reality. Once you see it was *built*, and recently, you can ask the forbidden question — *could it be built differently?* — without being laughed out of the room. Keep that question in your pocket. We open it fully in Lesson 24.

## The two values: a first taste

One more idea to plant now, because it will grow through the whole course. Things have (at least) two kinds of value, and the market only sees one.

- **Experiential value** — what something is worth *to you, in the having and doing of it*. A summer sunset. A friend helping you move house. A song that makes you cry. None of these has a price, and pricing them would *destroy* what they are.
- **Exchange value** — what something fetches in the market: its price.

A famous, real example: when researchers began *paying* people to donate blood, donations in some places went *down*. Paying turned a gift — an act rich in experiential value, in being-a-good-neighbour — into a cheap job, and people withdrew. **The act of putting a price on something can lower its true value.** The market, which sees only exchange value, is blind to this — and that blindness, scaled up to a whole society, is one of the deepest problems we will study. (Full treatment: Lesson 3.)

## The toolkit: how to read an economist without getting fooled

You now have the course's core stance. Let me make it operational. Whenever you meet an economic claim — in a newspaper, from a politician, from *me* — run it through five questions:

1. **Cui prodest? — Who benefits?** If this story were believed and acted on, who gains and who loses? "There is no alternative to austerity" is not a neutral forecast; it names winners (creditors) and losers (debtors).
2. **Is it description or prophecy?** Is the claim merely observing the world, or is it the kind of claim that helps *make* the world it describes (bank runs, confidence, "market sentiment")?
3. **What's held still?** Economists love the phrase *ceteris paribus* — "all else equal." But all else is never equal. Ask what had to be frozen for the conclusion to hold. (Method sidebar S3, later.)
4. **Society-with-markets or market-society reasoning?** Is the claim treating the market as one tool among many, or quietly assuming everything should be priced and sold?
5. **What kind of value is being counted — and what's invisible?** Is it counting only exchange value (prices, GDP) and ignoring experiential value, unpaid work, or the planet?

Keep these five in your pocket. By the capstone, you'll run them automatically.

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

| Term | In one honest sentence |
|------|------------------------|
| **Surplus** | More output than producers need to consume now — the seed from which money, debt, states, and hierarchy all grew. |
| **The economy** | How a society produces what it needs and decides who gets what — not merely "the money stuff." |
| **GDP** | The market value of what's bought and sold; useful, but blind to unpaid work and to nature. |
| **Society with markets** | An old, common arrangement where marketplaces are one tool among many. |
| **Market society** | A recent, made arrangement where almost everything — including labour and money — is bought and sold. |
| **Experiential value** | What something is worth in the living of it; often destroyed by pricing it. |
| **Exchange value** | What something fetches in the market: its price. |
| **Self-fulfilling prophecy** | A prediction that helps cause the outcome it predicts — why economics can't be value-free. |

## The takeaway

Economics is not a spectator sport played by experts in suits. It is the study of *who gets what, and who decides* — and because its stories help build the reality they describe, everyone with a stake in that reality (that is: everyone) has both a right and a duty to argue about it. You've swallowed the red pill. From here on, we don't ask "what is the law of the market?" We ask "*whose* law, serving *whom*, and could the story be told otherwise?"

Next lesson: we go back to the first granary, and watch money, debt, writing, priests, and kings all crawl out of a pile of surplus grain.
