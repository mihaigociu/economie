# Lesson 4 — Debt Came First, and Money After

## The hook: the barter story everyone believes, and no one can find

Open almost any economics textbook and you'll meet the same tidy creation myth. It goes like this:

> *Once upon a time, before money, people bartered. I had chickens, you had shoes; I wanted shoes, you wanted chickens, so we swapped. But barter was clumsy — what if the shoemaker didn't want my chickens? So, cleverly, humans invented money as a neutral medium to grease the swaps. Money is just a tool that made barter efficient.*

It's a lovely story. Adam Smith told a version of it in 1776, and economists have repeated it ever since. There is only one problem, and it is a big one:

**No anthropologist has ever found this barter economy. Anywhere. Ever.**

When historians and anthropologists went looking for the society that ran on chicken-for-shoes barter and then graduated to coins, they came back empty-handed. What they found instead, in community after community, was something the textbook never mentions: people ran their affairs on **credit and debt** — on webs of *"I'll remember I owe you."* The tidy sequence *barter → money → credit* is almost exactly backwards. The real sequence is **debt first, then money, and generalised barter mostly as a rare, awkward fallback** when trust breaks down (think of prisoners, or people in a collapsed currency).

Why does this matter enough to open a whole lesson with it? Because if you believe money came *first*, you'll think money is the natural, primary thing and debt is a scary derivative you should avoid. Once you see debt came first, you understand the truth that runs through the rest of this course: **money is not the opposite of debt. Money *is* debt — debt made to circulate.**

## How small communities really worked: the pub tab, scaled up

Picture a village, or the regulars in a pub. Nobody whips out coins for every round. Someone "puts it on the tab." A neighbour helps you fix a fence; you don't pay her — you *owe* her one, and everyone silently keeps score. A fisherman gives the baker fish today; the baker gives bread later. What's circulating isn't coins. It's **memory and obligation** — a running, informal ledger of who has done what for whom.

This is the true original economy, and it's built entirely of **debt**: not the frightening kind with bailiffs, but the ordinary connective tissue of *"I've got you, you've got me."* Anthropologist David Graeber, in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, showed this pattern across the world — and made a sharp point about the textbook's favourite problem.

The textbook says barter's fatal flaw is the **"double coincidence of wants"**: for a swap to happen, I must find someone who *has what I want* **and** *wants what I have* — a rare coincidence. True enough. But the textbook's solution (invent money) is not what humans actually did. What they actually did was simpler and older: **credit.** I take your shoes now and owe you; no coincidence of wants required, because the counter-gift can come later, in anything, from anyone in the web. Trust dissolves the double-coincidence problem long before coinage does.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Fine, but that only works in a small village where everyone knows everyone. Debt-by-memory can't run a city or a country. Isn't that exactly where money had to be invented?"*
>
> Precisely right — and that's the key to the whole thing. Money is what you get when a community grows too big for memory. When I can no longer *remember* that you owe me, or trust a stranger three towns over, we need to *write the debt down* and make the record **transferable** — so that a claim I hold against you can be handed to someone else in payment. That transferable, impersonal record of debt *is* money. It didn't replace debt with something new; it took the old village IOU and made it portable and anonymous. Money is memory for a society too large to remember.

## Money as debt made to circulate: the tally stick 🪵

Let me show you money being *literally* a piece of debt, in a system that ran England for over 500 years.

From medieval times into the 19th century, the English Exchequer recorded debts on **tally sticks** — notched pieces of wood. A debt (say, taxes a landholder owed the Crown, or money the Crown owed a supplier) was marked as notches along a stick, and then the stick was **split lengthwise** through the notches into two unequal halves:

- the longer piece, the **stock**, went to the creditor (this is where we get "stockholder");
- the shorter, the **foil**, stayed with the debtor as the matching record.

The two halves fit together only if genuine — an unforgeable receipt. Now here is the beautiful part: the creditor holding the *stock* — a claim on a future payment — could **spend it**. He could hand the tally to a third party to settle *his own* debts, and it would be accepted, because it represented a real claim that could eventually be redeemed. **A piece of debt, split from a stick, circulated as money.** People used these wooden IOUs to buy and sell for centuries. Money here isn't a shiny commodity that happens to be handy for trade — it is, visibly and physically, *a recorded debt that people pass around.*

## The deepest clue: the stone money of Yap 🗿

If the tally stick shows money *is* debt, the island of Yap shows what money *fundamentally is* — a shared ledger, not a thing.

On the Micronesian island of Yap, the traditional money was **rai**: enormous stone discs, some taller than a person, far too heavy to move. So when a rai changed owners — say, as payment — the stone *did not move at all*. It stayed exactly where it was. Everyone on the island simply *knew and agreed* that its ownership had transferred. The wealth wasn't in the stone's location; it was in the community's shared understanding of who owned what.

The famous punchline: one large rai had, generations earlier, sunk to the bottom of the sea during a storm while being ferried between islands. Nobody could see it or touch it. Yet the whole community continued to recognise its owner as wealthy and accepted transfers of that underwater stone as valid payment. **A stone no one could ever use again still functioned as money — because money was never really the stone. Money was the ledger in everyone's heads of who owed and owned what.**

Sit with that, because it demolishes our deepest intuition. We *feel* that money is a thing — a coin, a note, gold. Yap reveals the thing is just a token, a physical placeholder for the only thing that ever actually mattered: **a collectively agreed record of claims.** Which is to say — of debts.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"But gold, though. Gold is real money — it has value in itself, it's not just an IOU. Doesn't that break your whole argument?"*
>
> The best objection, and worth taking seriously. Gold is special because it's scarce, durable, and pretty, so it makes an unusually *good token* — a placeholder people across the world will accept, which is why it recurs in history. But notice: when you accept a gold coin for your labour, you don't want it for its glitter. You accept it because you trust that *someone else* will later accept it from you — that it's a claim you can cash in on others' future goods and labour. Even gold works as money only as a **socially-agreed claim on others**. Its metallic value is a *helpful crutch* for trust, not the source of its money-ness. Take away the shared agreement that others will accept it, and a gold coin buys you exactly what a sunken rai buys a stranger: nothing.

## So what *is* money, really?

Pulling the threads together, here is the definition this course will use — very different from the textbook's "medium of exchange that fixed barter":

> **Money is a transferable, socially-recognised record of debt — a claim on others' future goods and labour, held together by trust.**

Three consequences follow, and each seeds a later lesson:

1. **Money is a social relation, not a substance.** (Yap, the tally stick.) It exists in the space *between* people — in shared trust and record-keeping — not in the metal or paper itself. This is why its value can evaporate overnight if trust does (the self-fulfilling bank run, Lesson 10).

2. **If money is circulating debt, then whoever can issue trusted debt can create money.** Hold that thought. It is the entire secret of banking (Lesson 9): a bank makes a loan — creates a debt — and *that act creates new money*. Money isn't dug up; it's *promised into existence*.

3. **Money's value ultimately leans on the biggest, most trusted debtor of all — the state.** Why do we trust the state's money above all? A large clue: the state *demands its taxes be paid in that money, and no other.* That forces everyone to want it, giving it value from the top down. (Full treatment in Lesson 11.)

And carry forward the moral charge from Lesson 2. If money *is* debt, then the ancient bundling of debt with **guilt** (*Schuld*) is baked into money itself. This is why moralising language clings to finance — "sound money," "living within our means," a defaulting nation as a "delinquent." We'll watch that moral whip get used on an entire country in Part V.

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

|------|------------------------|
| **The barter myth** | The textbook tale that barter came first and money fixed it — a story anthropologists have never found evidence for. |
| **Double coincidence of wants** | Barter's problem (I must find someone who has what I want *and* wants what I have); solved historically by credit, not by inventing money. |
| **Credit / debt as origin** | The real primary economy: informal, remembered obligations (the "pub tab" scaled up). |
| **Tally stick (stock & foil)** | A split wooden record of debt whose creditor-half circulated as money for centuries — debt literally used as currency. |
| **Rai (stone money of Yap)** | Immovable (even sunken) stones that worked as money, proving money is a *shared ledger of claims*, not the physical token. |
| **Money (this course's definition)** | A transferable, socially-recognised record of debt — a trusted claim on others' future goods and labour. |

## The takeaway

The comforting textbook story — barter, then money, then credit — is backwards. **Debt came first**: real communities ran on remembered webs of obligation, the pub tab scaled up, long before coins. **Money is what debt becomes when a community grows too large to remember** — a transferable, impersonal record of who owes whom. The tally stick shows money *is* circulating debt; the sunken stone of Yap shows money is fundamentally a *shared ledger of claims*, never really the coin or note in your hand. This single re-founding — *money = debt made to circulate, held together by trust* — is the key that will later unlock banking (money created by lending), crises (trust evaporating), and the strange power of the state over the thing we thought was just a coin.

**Q1 — (b).** *Concept: the barter myth.* The tale isn't wrong because barter was efficient — it's wrong because the barter-first society it describes has never been found; the evidence points to credit/debt as the original economy, with generalised barter as a rare fallback when trust or currency breaks down.

**Q2 — False.** *Concept: money as a social relation.* Yap's *rai* (including one lost at the bottom of the sea) kept functioning as money though the stone never moved and couldn't even be seen — because money was the community's *shared ledger of who owns/owes what*, not the stone. **Q3.** *Concept: money as memory for large societies.* In a small group, obligations can be held in memory and enforced by everyone knowing everyone. As the community grows, you can't remember every debt or trust every stranger. So the debt must be **written down / tokenised** and, crucially, made **transferable** — a claim I hold against you must be handable to a third party in payment. That transferable, impersonal record of debt *is* money. *Key property to name: transferability (and impersonality/record-keeping).*

**Q4.** *Concept: tally stick / money = circulating debt.* A debt was notched on a stick, split into the creditor's **stock** and the debtor's **foil**. The stock — a claim on a *future* payment — could be **spent**: the creditor handed it to a third party to settle his own debts, and it was accepted because it represented a redeemable claim. So a literal piece of recorded debt passed from hand to hand as currency for centuries. This shows money is not a commodity that happens to be traded — it *is* transferable debt. *Strong answers state that the creditor's half circulated because it was a redeemable claim.*

**Q5.** *Concept: preview of endogenous money (L9).* Reasoning from "money = trusted, circulating debt": when a bank grants you a loan, it writes into your account a new deposit you can spend — a claim on real goods and labour that everyone accepts as money. But that deposit is simply the bank's *promise/IOU*; the bank didn't hand over pre-existing coins, it **created a new debt (yours to it, and its deposit-IOU to you) and that debt circulates as new money**. So lending doesn't move old money around — it *creates* money by creating trusted debt. *Full credit: connects loan = new debt = new money, without needing precise mechanics.*
