## The hook: the potato that doesn't sulk

Last lesson we watched Maria commit real money to an uncertain future. Today we zoom in on the strangest, most consequential item on her books — not the flour, not the ovens, but the **cooks and waiters**. Because when Maria "buys" their work, she is buying something that refuses, absolutely refuses, to behave like anything else she purchases.

Here's the difference, and I want you to feel it in your gut before we name it.

When Maria buys a **sack of potatoes**, the potatoes don't care. They don't work harder if she smiles at them or slower if she's rude. They don't get tired, resentful, inspired, or bored. They don't have a bad day. They don't organise with the other potatoes to demand better treatment. She pays the price, she owns the potatoes, and exactly the agreed quantity of "potato" is now hers. Done.

When Maria **rents a car**, same thing — it doesn't care who's driving; it delivers precisely its horsepower regardless of mood.

Now she hires **Andrei the cook**. She pays him €12 an hour. And here the whole logic breaks. Because what, *exactly*, has she bought for her €12?

She has *not* bought a fixed quantity of cooking, the way she bought a fixed quantity of potato. She has bought Andrei's *presence and willingness* for an hour. **How much actual cooking comes out of that hour — how fast, how carefully, how inventively — is not fixed by the price at all.** It depends on whether Andrei is motivated or sullen, rested or exhausted, treated with respect or contempt, watched or unwatched, hopeful about a raise or resigned to none. The €12 buys the *possibility* of work. The *actual* work is still to be fought for, every single hour.

That gap — between what the wage buys and what actually gets done — is one of the most important ideas in all of economics. It has a name, and once you see it you'll see it everywhere.

---

## The crucial distinction: labour-power vs labour

Here is the distinction the textbooks blur and Varoufakis (following Marx) insists you keep razor-sharp:

- **Labour-power** is what the employer actually buys: your *capacity to work* for a given time — your presence, your willingness to be directed, for that hour or that month. This is what the wage is the price of.
- **Labour** is what the employer actually *wants*: the real effort, care, and output your capacity gets *turned into* during that time.

> The employer pays for **labour-power** but needs **labour** — and *nothing in the wage contract guarantees how much of the second you'll get from the first.*

This is why labour is the commodity that isn't a commodity. With every other good, buying it *is* getting it — the potato is the potato. With labour-power, buying it is only *renting the person who might produce the labour*. The person comes attached: with a will, a mood, a body that tires, a sense of justice, and the capacity to resist. **You cannot separate the labour from the labourer** — which means you cannot ever fully *own* what you've paid for. You have to *extract* it.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"Isn't this just word-games? Andrei agreed to cook for €12 an hour. He should just... cook."*
>
> Ah, but *how much* cooking? The contract almost never says. It says "cook" — not "produce exactly 47 plates per hour at quality level 8." The employment contract is famously **incomplete**: it fixes the wage and the hours, but leaves the *intensity of effort* open, to be settled later, on the floor, by the daily push-and-pull between boss and worker. That "settled later" is not a footnote. It is the central drama of the workplace, and — as you're about to see — the hidden origin of both profit and conflict. Far from word-games, it's the one distinction that explains why workplaces look the way they do.

---

## Why this gap is the wellspring of profit

Now connect it to Lesson 6's third answer to "where does profit come from?"

Maria hires Andrei because she expects the *labour* she can extract from him to produce **more value than the labour-power costs her**. If his €12/hour of wages yields €30/hour of meals sold, the €18 gap is where her profit is drawn from. No employer hires anyone they expect to produce *less* than their wage — that's the whole logic of a job offer.

So the labour-power/labour gap isn't an accident or an abuse; it is the **structural engine of profit** under capitalism. This is the modern face of the surplus we met in the granary (Lesson 2): back then the surplus was grain skimmed by temple and king; now it is value skimmed through the wage relation — the difference between what workers *produce* and what their labour-power *costs*. Same surplus, new mechanism.

And here's the sharp edge: because profit is drawn from that gap, the employer has a permanent interest in **widening it** — extracting more labour (faster, longer, more careful work) for the same or less labour-power (the same or lower wage). The worker has the opposite interest. **The workplace is therefore a site of permanent, structural tension** — not because bosses are cruel or workers lazy, but because the two sides are pulling on the same gap from opposite ends.

---

## Why every workplace looks the way it does ⚡

Once you hold the labour-power/labour distinction, a hundred features of working life suddenly make sense. *All of them are machinery for closing the gap* — for turning purchased capacity into actual effort:

- **Bosses, supervisors, managers.** Someone must stand over the process making sure the labour-power actually becomes labour. A sack of potatoes needs no supervisor; a workforce does.
- **Monitoring and surveillance.** The stopwatch of "scientific management" (Taylorism, 1900s); the assembly line whose *speed* dictates yours; the call-centre that logs your every second; the Amazon warehouse scanner that times your walk between shelves; the delivery app tracking your route. Each is a technology for extracting maximum labour from bought labour-power.
- **Incentives and bonuses.** Piece-rates, commissions, "employee of the month," stock options — all attempts to make you *want* to close the gap yourself, so the boss doesn't have to.
- **Motivation, "culture," morale, fear.** From free office snacks to the threat of the sack — the whole apparatus of carrots and sticks exists because effort can't be bought outright, only elicited.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"So a football club is the perfect example — they pay a fortune for a star player's contract, but whether he actually runs his heart out is another matter entirely."*
>
> Exactly — you've got it precisely. The transfer fee and salary buy the player's **labour-power** (his availability, his obligation to play). Whether that becomes ninety minutes of blood-and-thunder **labour** depends on the manager, the bonuses, the fans, his pride, his mood, his contract-year ambitions. That's why clubs employ psychologists, motivators, and hard-man captains — a multi-million-euro industry devoted to the single problem of turning bought labour-power into delivered labour. The same problem faces the person managing a call-centre or a warehouse. Different pay grades, identical gap.

## Why the labour market won't clear like the potato market

Now the payoff — and the promise I made back in Lesson 5, that labour is one of the two "misbehaving" fictitious commodities. Here is *why* it misbehaves.

The supply-and-demand diagram (Sidebar S1) says: if there's a surplus of anything, the price falls until the surplus clears. Too many unsold potatoes? Drop the price; they sell. So — the mainstream concludes — if there's unemployment (a "surplus" of labour), just let wages fall until every worker is hired. Unemployment should be temporary and self-curing.

But it isn't. Mass unemployment persists for years, even with desperate people willing to work. Why won't the labour market clear? Three reasons, each flowing from what we've built:

1. **My wage is your customer's income.** This is the decisive one. A potato-seller who cuts his price doesn't reduce anyone's ability to buy potatoes. But if *all* employers cut wages at once, workers everywhere have less to spend — and workers are also *customers*. So cutting the "price of labour" shrinks the demand for the very goods labour produces. Maria's meals go unsold precisely because the neighbourhood's wages were cut. Lower wages can mean *fewer* jobs, not more. (This is the fallacy-of-composition that will sink austerity in Lesson 20, and the crisis mechanism of Lesson 10.)
2. **Labour can't be stored or withheld like potatoes.** You can't put your unsold labour-hours in a warehouse for next week; an hour not worked is gone forever. And you can't *stop eating* while you wait for a better price. So workers are forced to keep offering their labour even as its price falls — the "surplus" doesn't clear, it just gets cheaper *and* stays.
3. **The urgency asymmetry** (next section) means the price doesn't settle between equals.

So unemployment is not workers "choosing leisure at the going wage," as the tidy diagram implies. It is the predictable misbehaviour of a **fictitious commodity** that is really human lives — lives that must be sold to eat, can't be stockpiled, and whose collective price *is* the economy's demand.

## The hidden power imbalance: the urgency asymmetry ⚖️

One more thing the phrase "a wage freely agreed between equals" hides.

When Maria and Andrei negotiate, the law treats them as equal parties freely signing a contract. But look at what happens if they *don't* agree. Maria loses one candidate; she interviews others next week; her savings tide her over. Andrei, if he walks away, **doesn't eat**. Recall Lesson 5: the worker is "doubly free" — free of a master, and free of any land or capital to fall back on. He *must* sell his labour-power, this week, to survive. Maria, who owns the ovens, can usually *wait*.

That difference — **who can afford to wait** — is the **urgency asymmetry**, and it silently tilts every "free" negotiation. It's why, left alone, wages tend to be pushed toward the floor of what workers can bear rather than the ceiling of what they produce. And it's why the whole apparatus of the double movement (Lesson 5) — unions, minimum wages, unemployment insurance — exists: every one of those is a device to *reduce the worker's urgency*, to let him say "no" and wait a little, and thereby rebalance a negotiation that the word "free" pretends is already balanced.

> 🔴 **Red-pill callback.** "A freely agreed wage between equals" is a perfect specimen of the Lesson 1 ideology move: it takes a relationship shot through with *power* and redescribes it as a meeting of equals, so the outcome looks natural and fair. *Cui prodest?* — the party who can afford to wait. Whenever you hear "the labour market set this wage," ask: *set by whom, and who was hungrier when they signed?*

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

| Term | In one honest sentence |
|------|------------------------|
| **Labour-power** | Your *capacity to work* for a given time — the thing the wage actually buys. |
| **Labour** | The real effort and output that capacity gets turned into — the thing the employer actually wants. |
| **The labour-power / labour gap** | The open, contract-unspecified distance between what's bought (capacity) and what's extracted (effort) — the engine of both profit and workplace conflict. |
| **Incomplete contract** | An employment deal that fixes wage and hours but leaves *effort intensity* to be settled later, on the floor. |
| **Why the labour market won't clear** | Wages are also incomes (cutting them cuts demand), labour can't be stored, and the negotiation is unequal — so unemployment persists. |
| **Urgency asymmetry** | The worker must sell to eat *now*; the owner can usually wait — a hidden power gap inside every "free" wage bargain. |

## The takeaway

Labour is the commodity that isn't. When you hire a person you don't buy a fixed quantity of work the way you buy a sack of potatoes; you buy **labour-power** — a *capacity*, attached to a living human with a will, a body, and a sense of justice — and then you must *extract* the actual **labour** from it. The open gap between the two is the hidden engine of profit (value produced minus wages paid — the granary's surplus in modern dress) *and* the source of the permanent tension that fills workplaces with bosses, stopwatches, bonuses, and surveillance, all machinery for closing that gap. It is also why the labour "market" refuses to clear like a potato market: **wages are also incomes**, so cutting them cuts the very demand that creates jobs; labour can't be stockpiled; and the bargain is struck between a party who can wait and one who must eat. Strip away the phrase "a wage freely agreed between equals" and you find, underneath, the **urgency asymmetry** — power, wearing the mask of a fair contract.

**Q1 — (b).** *Concept: labour-power vs labour.* The wage buys *capacity* (labour-power); the effort/output actually produced (labour) is a separate, indeterminate thing that must be extracted. (c) and (d) are inventions; (a) is precisely the blur the lesson warns against.

**Q3.** *Concept: the gap as engine of profit and conflict.* Profit is drawn from the gap: employers hire only when the *labour* extracted is worth more than the *labour-power* (wage) paid — the surplus lives in that difference (the granary's surplus in wage form). But precisely because profit comes from widening the gap, the employer has a standing interest in extracting *more* effort for the *same/less* pay, while the worker has the opposite interest. Both are pulling on the same gap from opposite ends. Hence the tension is **structural** — built into the wage relation itself — not a product of individual cruelty or laziness; even a kind boss and a diligent worker face it. *Full credit: gap→profit AND gap→opposed interests AND the "structural, not personal" point.*

**Q4.** *Concept: wages are incomes / fallacy of composition.* The decisive reason: **a worker's wage is also someone's income and therefore someone's spending.** One potato-seller cutting his price doesn't reduce anyone's ability to buy potatoes — but if *all* employers cut wages together, total spending falls, goods go unsold, and firms hire *fewer* workers, not more. So economy-wide wage cuts can *deepen* unemployment. It connects to the **fallacy of composition** (what's true for one firm is false for all at once) — previewing the crisis mechanism (L10) and austerity (L20). Acceptable secondary points: labour can't be stored; the urgency asymmetry. *Full credit: the wages-are-incomes point named as primary.*

**Q5.** *Concept: urgency asymmetry + ideology.* Strong answers make these moves:

- **Ideology / cui prodest? (L1):** describing drivers as "free agents" reframes a power-laden employment relation as a meeting of equals, making the low rate look natural and freely chosen. It benefits the party setting the rate.
- **The mask of "free":** genuine freedom requires the ability to say "no" and wait — which the dispossessed "doubly free" worker (L5) lacks.
- **Double-movement device (name one):** a minimum wage/minimum per-trip rate, driver unionisation/collective bargaining, unemployment or gig-income insurance, or reclassification as employees with guaranteed pay — each reduces the driver's urgency and rebalances the bargain. *Full credit: urgency asymmetry applied + the "free" critique + one named remedy.*
