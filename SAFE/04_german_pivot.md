# Task 4 — Hypothesis H4: The German Auto-to-Defence Pivot

**Hypothesis (from research plan):** *Major German defence primes have measurably expanded capacity by absorbing automotive supply-chain assets (plants, suppliers, workforce) since 2023. Falsified if the expansion is greenfield or unrelated to automotive distress.*

**Date:** 2026-05-22
**Author:** Research note, Task 4 of 6
**Sister files:** `research_plan.md` (overall), `contracts_ledger.csv` (Romania ledger)

---

## Executive Summary

H4 is **partially confirmed, but with substantial qualification**. Three findings dominate:

1. The German automotive sector is in **systemic, regionally-concentrated distress** that began well before SAFE. Roughly **55,000–60,000 jobs** have been lost in the industry over 2023–2025, and a further **125,000 are projected to disappear by 2035**. VW will close at least three German plants for the first time in its 87-year history. Major suppliers (Bosch, ZF, Continental, Schaeffler) have announced cumulative cuts approaching **60,000 positions**. Cause: Chinese EV competition, collapse of German market share in China (25%→15%), high energy costs, and slow EV transition.

2. The German defence sector is **booming**, and a meaningful slice of that boom takes the form of *actual plant conversions and worker transfers from the auto sector to the arms sector*. Documented cases include KNDS's purchase of Alstom's Görlitz railcar plant (Saxony), KNDS's talks with Mercedes for the Ludwigsfelde Sprinter plant (Brandenburg), Rheinmetall's conversion of its own Berlin and Neuss component plants, Hensoldt's hiring of ~200 Continental/Bosch workers, and Volkswagen's talks with Rafael (Iron Dome). The Big Four German defence firms (Rheinmetall, Diehl, TKMS, MBDA Deutschland) **added ~16,500 employees since 2022** — a ~40% workforce expansion — with another 12,000 hires planned by end-2026.

3. **However**, most of the German defence build-up is still **greenfield or brownfield-but-internal** (new ammunition plants at Unterlüss, Lithuania, Bulgaria, Romania; new electronics capacity at Hensoldt; submarine yard expansion at TKMS Wismar). The number of *actual* auto-plant-to-tank-plant conversions remains in the **low single digits**. The Rheinmetall–VW Osnabrück deal — the iconic case — **collapsed in March 2026** with Rheinmetall declining to take over the plant. The "pivot" is real at the workforce level (Hensoldt-Continental, Rheinmetall-Continental MoU) and at a few high-profile plant level (Görlitz, prospective Ludwigsfelde), but quantitatively it is a small fraction of total defence-sector capacity growth.

**Verdict:** H4 is **confirmed in qualitative terms** (the pivot is real, named, documented) but **falsified in quantitative dominance terms** (the pivot is not the *main* mechanism of German defence expansion — greenfield ammunition factories, Eastern-European industrial network buildout, and the existing Wehrtechnik base are bigger). The "German bailout via SAFE" framing therefore overstates the auto-to-defence substitution while correctly identifying the broader political economy: SAFE money flows disproportionately to German primes (Rheinmetall in particular), at a time when those primes are the only large industrial absorber of displaced auto-sector skills.

A more precise framing: **SAFE is not a stealth bailout of German auto workers. It is a stealth subsidy of German prime contractors' order books, which happens to coincide with auto-sector pain that the same primes are absorbing in parts.**

**Ford Saarlouis**: closure of Focus production confirmed; partial conversion announced into a *pharmaceutical* site (not defence — important counter-example to the "everything is being converted to weapons" narrative).

**Mercedes Ludwigsfelde**: Sprinter chassis production scheduled to move to Poland by 2030. The vacated capacity is what KNDS is in talks about (see §3).

### 1.3 Regional concentration

Auto-industry distress is regionally concentrated, and these are also the regions where the defence-pivot narrative bites politically:

- **Lower Saxony** — VW (Wolfsburg, Osnabrück, Salzgitter, Emden); also Rheinmetall's Unterlüss home base, so the *same Land* hosts both the most-affected automotive workforce and the largest defence-sector expansion.
- **Baden-Württemberg** — Bosch (Stuttgart region), ZF (Friedrichshafen, Schweinfurt), Mercedes (Sindelfingen, Stuttgart); also Hensoldt (Ulm, Oberkochen), Diehl (Überlingen).
- **Saxony** — VW Dresden + Zwickau; also Görlitz (Alstom railcar plant just sold to KNDS).
- **Saarland** — Ford Saarlouis closure; relatively *less* defence offset.
- **Brandenburg** — Mercedes Ludwigsfelde (Sprinter, moving to Poland); KNDS interested.

The geographic overlap matters because politicians and the IG Metall union have framed defence expansion as the only credible "industrial replacement" jobs offer in regions where re-skilling to other sectors is genuinely difficult.

### 1.4 Why the auto sector is in trouble (causal stack)

1. **Chinese EV competition, both at home and at home-in-China.** German share in the Chinese market has roughly halved in five years, with German brands holding only ~5% of China's EV segment. Simultaneously, BYD, Geely, NIO, MG (SAIC), and Chery have built positive growth rates of 200–300% in EU registrations during 2024–2025.
2. **EV transition cost.** German manufacturers committed to electrification capex while domestic and European EV demand softened (subsidy withdrawal in Germany at end-2023, slower-than-projected ramp).
3. **Energy costs.** Post-2022 industrial electricity prices in Germany still elevated vs pre-crisis baseline, and well above US and Chinese benchmarks.
4. **US tariff overhang.** 2025 saw repeated tariff threats and partial implementation against EU-built vehicles, complicating German export economics.
5. **Slow product cycles vs Chinese OEMs** (German new-model cycles ~48 months vs Chinese ~18–24 months).
6. **Demand structural decline** in Europe: ~500,000 fewer VW Group unit sales/year vs pre-COVID baseline.

The worker-level pivot is real and named. But the absolute scale — ~200 confirmed transfers at Hensoldt, plus the soft Continental MoU at Rheinmetall, plus 500/month general hiring at Rheinmetall — is **modest relative to the ~55,000-60,000 net auto-sector job losses in 2024–2025 alone**. Even if Rheinmetall's *entire* 500/month hiring pipeline were auto-sector workers (it is not — many are engineering graduates, military veterans, etc.), that would be ~6,000/year against ~50,000+ annual auto-sector cuts. **The defence sector cannot absorb the auto-sector shock in workforce terms.** Rheinmetall, KNDS, Hensoldt management have said as much in public — they will hire selectively for skills they need; they are not a social-policy instrument.

### 3.3 The labour market mechanics — why the pivot is only partial

This subsection drills into *why* the auto-to-defence transfer cannot scale to absorb the displacement, even if both sides wanted it to.

**Skills mismatch:**
- Defence-sector demand is heaviest for: mechatronics technicians, welders certified for armor steel, high-precision machinists, electronics engineers, software/AI developers, optoelectronics specialists.
- Auto-sector displacement is heaviest in: ICE engine components (Pierburg, Mahle), powertrain assembly (ZF), tire production (Continental), and general assembly-line operators.
- Overlap is *strongest* in mechatronics, electronics, and welding. The Hensoldt-Continental Wetzlar transfer (auto electronics → defence electronics) and the Renk hiring from auto-transmissions both exploit *exactly this overlap*. KNDS's interest in Mercedes Ludwigsfelde is partly about the Sprinter assembly-line workforce being immediately useful for Boxer wheeled vehicle assembly (which has more structural similarity to a heavy van than to a tank).
- Overlap is *weakest* in ICE-engine assembly (pure-displacement risk, no defence demand) and tire production (Continental Reifen has no defence analogue).

**Security clearance bottleneck:**
- The BDSV repeatedly flags security clearance times as a structural friction. A worker moving from VW Wolfsburg to Rheinmetall Unterlüss may face a 3–6 month clearance gap during which they cannot start.
- The German Verfassungsschutz (BfV) clearance process is paper-heavy and slow. As of mid-2026 there is no announced reform to accelerate it.

**Geographic mismatch:**
- VW Wolfsburg → Rheinmetall Unterlüss: 60 km (workable).
- Bosch Stuttgart → Hensoldt Ulm: 90 km (workable; Hensoldt is actively organising for this).
- ZF Friedrichshafen → KNDS Munich: 200 km (difficult without relocation).
- Mercedes Sindelfingen → KNDS Kassel: 350 km (impractical without relocation).
- Mercedes Ludwigsfelde → KNDS facility (in Brandenburg): same-site transfer if Ludwigsfelde deal closes — this is part of why that deal matters.

**Wage and culture differential:**
- Defence pays comparably to auto for similar grades, but the cultural shift (security clearance, classification, export-control compliance) is real. IG Metall has noted that many auto workers are *reluctant* to move into weapons production for political/ethical reasons, even before considering practicalities.
- Younger workers, university-graduate engineers, and ex-Bundeswehr personnel are easier defence hires than mid-career auto-line assemblers.

**Quereinsteiger (career-changers):**
- German press has tracked the rise of *Quereinsteiger* into defence in late 2025 / 2026. Defence firms market explicitly to auto-sector workers via job-fair targeting (the Hensoldt Wetzlar visit being the case-study).
- But the headline absorption numbers (Hensoldt 200, KNDS 2,000 prospective at Ludwigsfelde, ~350 at Görlitz, Rheinmetall 500/month *of which a fraction is auto*) sum to perhaps **5,000–8,000 cross-sector transfers** in 2024–2026 combined, against ~55,000+ auto-sector job losses over the same window.

### 3.4 Government policy and union positioning

- **IG Metall (Lower Saxony branch):** Has observed Rheinmetall increasingly collaborating with the automotive industry to absorb skilled workers. *Publicly cautious*: warned about "one-sided industrial policy focus towards rearmament" and that short-term employment in defence is "based on an unstable security environment." The union's official line: support workers, but do not endorse a structural defence-substitution model.
- **VDA–IG Metall joint statement (September 2025):** "Secure jobs in the automotive industry now." Explicitly *defends* the auto sector and requests transition support; does not propose defence conversion as a policy answer.
- **BDSV (Federation of German Security and Defence Industries):** CEO Hans Atzpodien has argued government must support transition via retraining funding, relocation cost coverage, and faster security clearances (which he calls a "key bottleneck"). The BDSV's position is the most pivot-friendly, but it is industry advocacy, not policy.
- **BMWK (Economic Affairs Ministry):** Auto-transition policy and defence-industrial policy are still run as **separate workstreams** institutionally. There is no published BMWK programme of "auto-to-defence reallocation". The closest is the 2024 working paper from BMWK on defence-industrial strategy, which mentions skill transferability without making it a central pillar.
- **BMVg (Defence Ministry):** Procurement policy emphasis is on Bundeswehr capability and EU joint procurement, not on workforce sourcing.

In short: the *political* framing of "auto-to-defence pivot" is more enthusiastic in media (and in the BDSV) than in government documents or union positions.

### 3.5 The Volkswagen factor

VW is the most-watched data point because of its symbolic weight:
- **Refused** to host weapons production at Osnabrück (Rheinmetall talks failed).
- **In talks** with Rafael (Israel) about Iron Dome trucks, launchers, and power generators at Osnabrück (12–18 months conversion timeline if approved). VW has reiterated the "no interceptor missiles" red line. This is **defence-adjacent**, not core weapons.
- VW has *not* announced any other defence-conversion deal as of May 2026.

The Spiegel/Handelsblatt-cited "VW workers being recruited by Rheinmetall in Lower Saxony" stories tend to be **anecdotal individual cases**, not a structured programme. They are real but small.

### 3.6 Mercedes signal

May 2026: Mercedes-Benz CEO Ola Källenius told the *Wall Street Journal* that the company is "open" to defence production if "economically viable" — but characterised it as a "negligible share" and a possibly "growing niche." This is *signalling*, not a commitment. Combined with the Ludwigsfelde-KNDS talks, it suggests Mercedes is exploring an exit-from-vans into defence partnership, but the structure is not yet defined.

### 4.4 Estimating Germany's overall SAFE share — rough magnitude

A genuine ledger of all SAFE-funded contract awards across 19 states is months from being complete. But we can bound it from above and below.

**Upper bound (Romania-pattern projected EU-wide):** If Romania's ~55% Rheinmetall share applied to every state, German primes would absorb ~€80 bn of €150 bn. This is implausible.

**Lower bound (Poland-pattern projected EU-wide):** If Tusk's 0.37% applied everywhere, German primes would absorb ~€0.55 bn. Equally implausible.

**Realistic estimate (judgement, not data):**
- Romania (~€16.7 bn): German share ~€7–9 bn (Rheinmetall + smaller German content elsewhere). Call it 45–55%.
- Hungary (~€16.2 bn): German share via Rheinmetall Hungary JV ~50% of Lynx-related volume → ~€3–5 bn.
- Italy (~€14.9 bn): German share via LRMV JV, plus some Diehl/Hensoldt sensors → ~€2–3 bn (15–20%).
- France (~€16.2 bn): German share via KNDS shared structure ~€1–2 bn (10–15%).
- Poland (~€43.7 bn): German share per Tusk ~0.37% → ~€0.16 bn. Even if Tusk understates by an order of magnitude, ≤ ~€1.5 bn.
- Belgium, Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia (~€23 bn aggregate): mixed. Germany strong in Lithuania (KNDS Leopard 2A8 final assembly, Rheinmetall ammo plant). German share 25–40% → ~€6–9 bn.
- Smaller states (Spain, Czechia, Croatia, Slovakia, Bulgaria, Cyprus, Greece, Denmark, Portugal, Finland — ~€18 bn aggregate): German share variable, likely 20–35% → ~€4–6 bn.

**Indicative total: €23–35 bn for German primes out of €150 bn — i.e. ~15–25% of SAFE.**

This is *above* Germany's share of EU GDP (~25%) only at the upper end, and *roughly proportionate* at the lower end. It is *not* a "German bailout" in headline-share terms across the whole programme — but it is *highly concentrated* in specific states (Romania, Hungary, Lithuania) where the German prime share is dominant.

The 88-bn-figure for Rheinmetall (mentioned in the prompt) is the **total nomination potential across all of Rheinmetall's customer base, not SAFE-specific.** It includes Bundeswehr direct contracts, NATO clients, Ukraine, exports, US business. The SAFE-attributable slice is probably **€8–12 bn** of that potential (much of it Romania + Hungary + Lithuania ammo plant + Bulgaria ammo plant + some Italian LRMV flow). The Rheinmetall non-SAFE pipeline is bigger than its SAFE pipeline.

### 4.5 The Tusk 0.37% claim, contextualised

Tusk's number is **rhetorical and accurate as far as Polish-side procurement data show, but designed for political defence against the PiS "German leash" framing**. Three caveats:

1. The 0.37% counts *direct German prime* contract value, not the subsystem content of Polish-prime products. A Polish K2 tank may carry German-content subsystems (rangefinders from Hensoldt, transmission elements from Renk) that are not captured.
2. The 0.37% excludes the German-Swedish-Italian content embedded in joint platforms.
3. The 0.37% is *Polish-specific* and cannot be generalised. Poland is the *outlier* — most other SAFE states have higher German-prime exposure.

So Tusk is right *for Poland*, and wrong as a general claim about SAFE. The Romanian counter-pattern (~55% to one German firm) is the more striking case.

## Section 5 — Verdict on H4

### 5.1 Hypothesis recap

> **H4:** Major German defence primes have measurably expanded capacity by absorbing automotive supply-chain assets (plants, suppliers, workforce) since 2023. Falsified if the expansion is greenfield or unrelated to automotive distress.

### 5.2 Evidence weighed

**For H4 (the pivot is real):**
- ✅ Hensoldt has *committed in writing* to take on ~200 Continental and Bosch auto workers (January 2025) — the cleanest documented worker transfer.
- ✅ KNDS *acquired* Görlitzer Waggonbau from Alstom (Saxony, Feb 2025) and is transitioning ~580 of 700 workers; this is a railcar plant, not auto strictly, but it is a civilian-industrial-to-defence conversion in a region (Saxony) with severe auto stress.
- ✅ KNDS is *in advanced talks* with Mercedes for Ludwigsfelde (Brandenburg) — ~€1 bn, ~2,000 Mercedes workers transferred, motivated by Boxer order pipeline.
- ✅ Rheinmetall has *internally converted* its own Berlin and Neuss auto-component plants to defence production (operational since July 2025 in Berlin).
- ✅ Rheinmetall has *signed* an MoU with Continental (June 2024) for retraining auto workers.
- ✅ Renk Augsburg is *actively recruiting* from the auto sector for transmissions skills (CNBC, May 2025).
- ✅ Volkswagen is *in talks* with Rafael (Iron Dome) for Osnabrück conversion (defence-adjacent).
- ✅ Mercedes CEO has *publicly signalled* willingness to enter defence production (May 2026).
- ✅ German defence Big Four added *16,500+ jobs* since 2022; +12,000 planned by end-2026.
- ✅ Rheinmetall application volume from Germany tripled (59,000 → 175,000) between 2021 and 2024.
- ✅ The *geographic overlap* of auto distress and defence-prime presence is striking (Lower Saxony, Baden-Württemberg, Saxony, Brandenburg).

**Against H4 (the pivot is small relative to the broader expansion):**
- ❌ The *most-publicised* pivot case — Rheinmetall taking over VW Osnabrück — **failed** in March 2026.
- ❌ Rheinmetall's flagship capacity expansion is at **Unterlüss** (greenfield-on-existing-campus, 350,000 shells/year), **Lithuania** (greenfield), **Bulgaria** (greenfield, €1 bn), **Romania** (greenfield, €500 m+), **Ukraine** (greenfield JV), **US** (greenfield). Of these, none is an auto-plant conversion.
- ❌ KNDS's Norway plant (Levanger) is greenfield. KNDS's existing Munich, Kassel, Kiel facilities are being expanded on-site.
- ❌ Hensoldt's €1 bn capacity investment is *additive* to existing Ulm/Oberkochen — not a conversion.
- ❌ TKMS Wismar expansion (1,500 jobs by 2029) is shipyard, not auto.
- ❌ The Bundeswehr Sondervermögen (€100 bn) and Sondervermögen-2 (€500 bn) are the **larger funding lever** than SAFE for German defence growth, and German *did not apply for SAFE*. The "SAFE = German bailout" framing is therefore loose.
- ❌ Rheinmetall is *divesting* (not absorbing) civilian automotive supply (Pierburg Pump Technology, Kolbenschmidt, the entire Power Systems segment) — 400+ Pierburg workers headed for a third-party buyer, not for tank production.
- ❌ Ford Saarlouis converted to *pharmaceuticals*, not defence — a useful counter-example showing not every closed auto plant goes to weapons.
- ❌ Total worker absorption from auto → defence in documented cases is on the order of *low thousands* (Hensoldt 200, KNDS Görlitz 350–400, KNDS Ludwigsfelde 2,000 prospective, Rheinmetall Berlin/Neuss workforces). Against ~55,000 auto sector jobs lost in 2024–2025 alone, the absorption is **roughly 5–10% of the displacement**.

### 5.3 Verdict

**H4 is partially confirmed — but not in the "stealth bailout" sense.**

- The auto-to-defence pivot is **real, named, documented, and accelerating**.
- It is also **a minority component** of German defence expansion. The dominant mode is greenfield ammunition, expansion at existing defence-dedicated sites (Unterlüss, Ulm, Wismar, Munich/Kassel), and Eastern-European industrial buildout (Lithuania, Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, Ukraine).
- The pivot is **most credible at the worker-level (Hensoldt, Renk, Rheinmetall MoU with Continental)** and at a **handful of plant-level cases** (Görlitz signed; Ludwigsfelde pending; Berlin/Neuss internal). It is *not* visible as a system-wide industrial-policy reallocation.
- Workforce-wise, the defence sector *cannot* absorb the auto-sector shock at scale. ~12,000 planned defence hires through end-2026 against ~50,000+ annual auto-sector losses → 20–25% absorption ratio at best.
- SAFE itself is **not the lever** of the pivot. Germany did not apply for SAFE. The relevant German funding levers are the Sondervermögen (€100 bn, 2022) and Sondervermögen-2 (€500 bn, 2025). SAFE *flows to German primes via other states' procurement* (Romania especially, also Hungary, Lithuania, parts of Italy).

### 5.4 The "stealth bailout" thesis, weighed

The claim that SAFE is a stealth bailout for German industry hit by Chinese EV competition is **a half-truth**:

- **The half that's true:** SAFE *does* channel disproportionate shares to German primes (Rheinmetall above all) in several large-allocation states. Rheinmetall's order book benefits enormously. The German defence industry *is* absorbing some auto-sector workers and capacity, and the political-economy logic — "Germany loses one strategic industry, builds another" — is recognisable in policy discourse.
- **The half that's misleading:** Germany did not apply for SAFE. Most of the German defence expansion is *non-SAFE-funded* (Bundeswehr Sondervermögen, exports, NATO-state direct procurement). The auto-to-defence absorption is real but small in absolute scale relative to the displacement. And the SAFE-funded German prime share is *concentrated in a few states*, not uniform across the program — Tusk's Poland is at one extreme (0.37%) and Romania at the other (~55%).

A more accurate framing: **SAFE is a Romania/Hungary/Lithuania subsidy to Rheinmetall et al., not a Brussels-engineered transfer of auto-sector pain into defence-sector gain.** The auto pain and the defence gain are happening at similar times in similar regions in Germany, with overlapping firms and people, but the *causal link via SAFE specifically* is weak. The Sondervermögen is the bigger causal lever.

### 5.5 Confidence and uncertainty

- **High confidence:** the macro facts (auto job losses, defence company financials, SAFE allocations by country, named plant deals).
- **Medium confidence:** the magnitude of Rheinmetall's SAFE share (because per-state ledgers are still incomplete for most states — Romania is the only one with substantial public detail).
- **Low confidence:** the *total* worker absorption count from auto to defence — only the named cases are documented; informal individual moves are unmeasured.
- **Uncertainty range for "German prime share of total SAFE pool":** ~15–25%. (15% if Tusk-like patterns dominate; 25% if Romania-like patterns dominate in mid-allocation states.)
- **Direction of revision:** as more state-level SAFE plans publish detail (especially Italy, Belgium, Latvia, Lithuania) over 2026–2027, this estimate can tighten.

### 5.6 The SAFE 65%-content rule and the German prime advantage

A structural feature of SAFE worth surfacing here for H4:

- SAFE Regulation requires **≥65% of end-product value to originate in EU + EEA-EFTA + Ukraine**.
- This rule was *intended* by EU negotiators to favour European industry over US suppliers. In practice it also **favours large incumbent European primes over smaller national champions** in mid-sized member states.
- A Romanian buyer wanting a tracked IFV in 2025–2026 has, realistically, three EU-compliant options that meet the 65% rule at scale: Rheinmetall Lynx, KNDS Boxer/Puma, GDELS-Mowag Piranha (Swiss, with German/Italian content). Hanwha Redback (Korean) fails the rule without major Polonisation/Romania-localisation. The 65% rule is *not the same as* a "German content" rule, but in practice it advantages German primes because they are the largest EU-resident producers.
- This is a structural advantage that interacts with the H4 pivot story: even if German auto workers are *not* pivoting to defence in volume, the German prime *firms* still win the SAFE order book by structural design.
- A precise way to put it: the German bailout, if there is one, runs through **the 65% rule + German prime scale**, not through the auto-pivot mechanism. The auto-pivot is a *political legitimation narrative* (Germany loses one strategic industry, builds another, soaks up displaced workers); the actual economic mechanism is much more boring (large European primes win European procurement at preferential loan terms).

### 5.7 What would *falsify* H4 more strongly?

Worth being explicit about the asymmetric reasoning here. The hypothesis as stated is somewhat under-specified, so let me list what would push the verdict.

**Stronger confirmation would require:**
- Net auto-to-defence worker transfer **>20% of auto-sector job losses** (currently ~10%).
- Conversion of **≥5 named major auto plants** to defence production (currently 2–3 confirmed + 1 pending + 1 failed).
- Explicit BMWK industrial-policy programme reallocating auto-displaced labour to defence (currently informal/MoU-based).
- German prime SAFE share aggregated across the program **>40%** (currently 15–25%).

**Stronger falsification would require:**
- All German defence capacity growth being demonstrably greenfield with no auto-pivot component (currently false — there are 3+ documented plant cases).
- Auto-sector job losses being absorbed primarily by *other* sectors (e.g. solar, hydrogen, healthcare) — partially true (Ford Saarlouis → pharma) but defence is visibly absorbing a chunk.
- German prime SAFE share remaining low across all states (currently false for Romania, Hungary, Lithuania).

The actual evidence sits **squarely in the middle**, which is why the verdict is "partially confirmed with substantial qualification."

### 5.8 Competing hypotheses (alternative interpretations)

The H4 narrative is one of three plausible framings. The data fit all three to varying degrees:

1. **"Auto-to-defence pivot" (H4 as stated).** German defence growth is partly fed by displaced auto capacity and labour. Status: confirmed for ~10–20% of the defence expansion; rejected as the *dominant* driver.

2. **"German Sondervermögen drives everything; SAFE is incidental."** The 2022 €100 bn Bundeswehr fund and 2025 €500 bn debt-brake reform are the *real* drivers of German defence-industrial growth; SAFE is a peripheral mechanism that channels some additional orders. Status: largely accurate. The Sondervermögen is bigger than the German-prime SAFE share.

3. **"SAFE is a structural subsidy to incumbent EU primes regardless of nationality."** The 65% rule + state-by-state procurement choice + pre-existing industrial concentration means SAFE *was always going to flow disproportionately to large incumbents*, which happen to be German (Rheinmetall, KNDS, Hensoldt) and French (Naval Group, Thales, Dassault). Status: most consistent with the data. The "German" framing is partial: Italy gets Leonardo flow, France gets KNDS-France/Naval Group flow, Sweden gets Saab flow (large Polish content). German share is high because German primes are large, not because SAFE was designed to favour Germany specifically.

The objective synthesis should probably foreground (3), with (2) as the German-side complement and (1) as the politically resonant but quantitatively secondary mechanism.

### 5.9 Implications for the wider research plan

For the synthesis (research plan §7):
- H1 (German share > 40% of *Romania*'s SAFE) is **likely confirmed** — Romania-specific data point to 45–55% to German primes (mostly Rheinmetall).
- H4 (auto-to-defence pivot) is **partially confirmed** as above, but should not be conflated with H1. The H1 picture (German prime dominance) is much stronger than the H4 picture (auto-conversion mechanism). H1 is true mostly because German primes are *large, available, and pre-positioned*, not because they are absorbing auto capacity — though they are also doing the latter, modestly.
- The "Polish counterfactual" (Tusk's 0.37%) is informative for Romania: it shows that high domestic content is *politically possible* but requires a pre-existing defence industrial base (PGZ, HSW, Mesko) and political will to use it. Romania's lack of a comparable base — ROMARM is small and Soviet-era-dominant — is what drives the high German prime share.
- The Romanian industrial offset (40% local Lynx content via Automecanica Mediaș; Rheinmetall–Pirochim gunpowder JV; potential ammunition co-production) is the *partial* compensation. Whether it adds up to a credible industrial-policy upside for Romania is for Task 6 (macro/fiscal) and Task 5 (transparency) to weigh.

### 5.10 What this means for the "German bailout" charge

In summary, the "SAFE is a German auto-sector bailout via defence orders" framing is **mostly wrong as stated** but **partially right in a way that matters**.

Wrong because:
- Germany did not apply for SAFE. Germany pays for its defence growth out of the Sondervermögen.
- Most of the German defence-sector growth is *not* auto-conversion. It is greenfield (Unterlüss, Lithuania, Bulgaria, Romania), brownfield-internal (Hensoldt Ulm, KNDS Munich/Kassel), or M&A (NVL, Loc Performance, Hagedorn-NC) — none of which is "absorbing displaced VW workers."
- The number of German auto workers who have actually been hired by German defence firms is in the **low thousands**, against ~55,000+ auto-sector job losses in 2024–2025.
- The most-publicised pivot case (Rheinmetall–VW Osnabrück) failed.

Right in a way that matters because:
- A subset of states (Romania, Hungary, Lithuania) is sending the majority of its SAFE allocation to German primes. For Romania specifically, the figure is ~50%+ flowing to one company (Rheinmetall).
- This is happening at the exact time that German defence primes are scaling fastest in their history, with Rheinmetall's order book quadrupling between 2022 and 2026 and the Big-Four defence workforce growing 40%.
- The political-economy *narrative* of "Germany loses cars, builds tanks" is sticky and has been embraced by some industry voices (BDSV) and rejected by others (IG Metall) — it has policy weight even where the empirical reality is thinner.
- For Romania, the right question is not "is SAFE a German bailout?" but "is Romania getting good value for a 45–55% Rheinmetall concentration, vs. counterfactuals it could have pursued (open competition with Hanwha, GDELS, or a Polish co-production model)?" That question belongs to Task 3 (Lynx/Hanwha thread) and Task 6 (macro/fiscal).

## Appendix C — Continental and Bosch as dual-use technology cases

A theme not fully developed in the main text deserves a short note: **Continental and Bosch are not just labour-supply pools for the defence pivot — they have technology stacks that are directly dual-use.** This matters because it changes the H4 interpretation: the pivot is not only about reallocating workers from one industry to another, but also about transferring (and in some cases re-purposing) the IP they built.

**Continental's dual-use stack:**
- **Automotive radar / ADAS sensors.** Continental's 4D imaging radars (77 GHz) for ADAS share architecture with defence-grade short-range surveillance radars (think counter-drone, perimeter security). Hensoldt explicitly hired Continental electronics-radar engineers for this reason — *the skill transfers in days, not months*.
- **Sensor fusion / computer vision.** Continental's autonomous-driving stack has direct dual-use in unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs), counter-UAS optical tracking, and battlefield situational awareness.
- **Tyres.** Less dual-use in obvious ways, but Continental does have a defence-tyre programme (run-flat technology for military vehicles).
- **Power electronics / inverters.** Used in EV powertrains; directly applicable to electric military vehicles, drones, and electric weapons (e.g. laser-system power supplies).

**Bosch's dual-use stack:**
- **MEMS sensors (Bosch Sensortec).** Tiny accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers, pressure sensors — used in mobile phones and cars, but also the workhorse sensors in *every quadcopter drone ever made*. Bosch is one of the largest MEMS suppliers globally.
- **Counter-drone systems.** Bosch filed a 2024 US patent for a UAS-based counter-drone system using electromagnetic jamming. This is a *direct* Bosch defence move.
- **Power electronics.** EV inverters / motor controllers transfer almost 1:1 to electric tank propulsion and electric drone propulsion.
- **Diesel injection, common-rail.** Bosch's traditional diesel-engine technology supports military trucks, generators, ship propulsion.
- **Engine control units (ECUs).** Reprogrammable; defence variants exist.

**Implication for H4:** the auto-to-defence transition is partly a *technology transfer* (Continental and Bosch IP becoming defence IP) rather than just a *worker transfer*. This is a more credible long-run mechanism than the headline plant conversions, and it is largely invisible in public reporting because it happens via individual hires, MoUs, and product roadmaps rather than via plant deals. The CNAS report "A Perspective on Germany — Proliferated Drones" (2024) is one of the few public documents that tracks this dimension.

**Caveat:** dual-use is not the same as dual-revenue. Continental's defence revenue remains a tiny fraction of its automotive revenue. Bosch has not announced a defence segment in its financial reporting. The IP transfers, but the corporate identities remain auto-sector. This is the "Mercedes signal" pattern — *willingness to consider* defence at a "negligible share of portfolio" without actually entering the segment seriously.

## Appendix D — Macroeconomic context

A few macro data points worth landing for the synthesis:

**German auto sector as share of economy:**
- Direct employment: ~761,000 (2024).
- GDP contribution: ~5% (direct + first-order suppliers).
- Export contribution: ~17% of German goods exports.

**German defence sector as share of economy:**
- Direct employment: ~105,000 (2024–2025).
- GDP contribution: ~0.8% (BMWK estimate, 2024).
- Growing fast, but **scale-wise still ~7× smaller than auto in employment terms**.

**Net employment effect of pivot:**
- If German defence Big-Four adds ~12,000 jobs by end-2026 (announced), plus Rheinmetall's broader 8,000-over-two-years plan, plus Hensoldt's 200 specifically from Continental/Bosch, plus KNDS's projected ~2,000 from Mercedes Ludwigsfelde, plus TKMS Wismar's 1,500, that totals roughly **20,000–25,000 new German defence jobs through 2027–2029**.
- Against auto-sector cuts of *announced* 60,000+ by 2030 (VW alone 35,000; Bosch 22,000; ZF 14,000; Continental 3,000; smaller suppliers 10,000+).
- **Net absorption: ~30–40%** even on optimistic interpretation.
- **Net absorption of actual cross-sector transfers (not just total defence hires): ~10–15%.**

**The macro upshot:** the defence sector is *too small to bail out the auto sector*. Even if every defence job were filled by a former auto worker (which is not the case), the absorption ratio caps out at maybe 30%. Germany needs other industrial answers (solar, hydrogen, battery cells, AI services) for the bulk of the auto workforce. Defence is **a politically useful absorption story, not a macroeconomically sufficient one.**

This is the strongest single argument against the "stealth bailout" framing: the math doesn't add up. The German defence sector simply isn't big enough to absorb the German auto sector's pain at scale. SAFE flows to German primes are a *windfall* for those primes, but they don't translate into a *bailout* of the displaced auto workforce.

## Appendix E — Methodology and limitations

**Data sources used:**
- Company annual reports and quarterly statements (Rheinmetall, KNDS, Hensoldt, TKMS, Diehl, Renk).
- SIPRI Top-100 Arms-Producing Companies, 2024 fact sheet (November 2025).
- VDA workforce statistics and Prognos employment study (2024, 2026).

- Romanian: HotNews, G4Media, Bursa.ro, Defense Romania.
- Polish: Notes from Poland, Breaking Defense, Brussels Signal; PM Tusk's Sejm remarks.

**Limitations of this analysis:**
- Per-state SAFE contract-by-contract data is **incomplete** for most states as of May 2026. Romania is the best-documented; Poland's broad strokes are documented (via Tusk's defence in the Sejm); other states are partial.
- The "auto-to-defence pivot" cases below 500 workers are unsystematically tracked. The ledger in Appendix A captures the named, reported cases but likely undercounts individual moves.
- "German prime share" attribution is complicated by JVs: LRMV (50/50 Rheinmetall/Leonardo) is half-German economically. Rheinmetall Hungary (51/49 with Hungarian state) is majority-German legally, but the Hungarian state captures the public-policy upside. KNDS is binational.
- The €88 bn figure for Rheinmetall potential orders is **not SAFE-specific**. It is total order pipeline (Bundeswehr + NATO + Ukraine + exports + SAFE indirectly). Quoting it as a SAFE-attributable figure (as some Romanian press does) is misleading.
- Forward-looking statements (e.g. KNDS Ludwigsfelde, Rheinmetall–Lockheed JV) are not yet contractually closed and may not materialise as described.

**Reviewer's note for the synthesis:**
- H4 should be reported as **partially confirmed with substantial caveats**, not as a clean "yes." The pivot exists; it is not the dominant mode.
- Do not let press headlines about Rheinmetall taking over VW Osnabrück drive the narrative: that deal **failed** in March 2026.
- Keep the German-prime-share estimate honest: it is 45–55% for Romania, 50%+ for Hungary and Lithuania, but ~0.4% for Poland, ~10–15% for France and Italy. There is no single "German share of SAFE" number — it is state-by-state.
