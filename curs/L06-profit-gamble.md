## The hook: the restaurant that spends before it sells

Welcome to Part II. In Part I we assembled the machine — surplus, the two values, money-as-debt, the market society. Now we switch it on and watch it *run*. And the first thing that runs is the most familiar and least understood word in the whole subject: **profit.**

Let me start you inside a restaurant that hasn't opened yet.

The owner — call her Maria — has signed a lease. Before a single customer walks in, she must **pay, in real money, now**: the rent, the deposit on the ovens, the first delivery of flour and tomatoes and fish, the wages of the cooks and waiters for the opening weeks. Thousands of euros go *out* the door before a single euro comes *in*. And here is the knife-edge she stands on: she has *no idea* whether anyone will come. Maybe the neighbourhood loves her. Maybe it rains for a month. Maybe a better restaurant opens across the street. She has committed real resources to a future that has not happened yet and *might not*.

That knife-edge is the beating heart of capitalism, and almost every textbook walks right past it. So slow down and see it clearly:

> **Under capitalism, costs come *first* and revenue comes *later* — if it comes at all. Production is a bet placed across time, in the dark.**

This one structural fact — the *reversal of time*, cost before revenue, commitment before knowledge — is where profit, risk, debt, competition, and crisis all come from. Get it into your bones now and half of Part II will feel inevitable.

---

## Where does profit actually come from?

Profit is simply the gap:

> **Profit = revenue (what customers pay) − costs (what you paid to produce).**

Easy to write. But *why does the gap exist at all*, and where does the money in it come from? This is one of the oldest and most contested questions in economics, and I'll give you the three great answers — because which one you believe shapes your whole politics.

**Answer 1 — Profit is the reward for risk.** Maria bore the uncertainty; she might have lost everything. Profit is her prize for shouldering a gamble the cooks and waiters (who got paid regardless) did not. *There's real truth here* — the gamble is real, as we just saw.

**Answer 2 — Profit is the reward for "abstinence" / for organising.** Maria didn't spend her savings on a holiday; she tied them up in ovens and waited. Profit rewards her patience and her skill at organising land, labour, and capital into a working whole. *Also partly true* — organising production is genuine work.

**Answer 3 — Profit comes from labour producing more value than it is paid.** This is the answer the textbooks bury, and the one Varoufakis (following Marx) insists you at least *understand*, whether or not you end up agreeing. It goes like this: the cooks and waiters, over a shift, produce goods and services that sell for **more** than their wages. That difference — the **surplus** they create but do not receive — is the wellspring the profit is drawn from. On this view, profit isn't conjured from risk or patience alone; it is the **surplus of Part I** (remember the granary!) reappearing in modern dress, now extracted through the wage relation rather than handed to a temple.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"That last one sounds like an accusation — that profit is 'stolen' from workers. Isn't profit just the fair reward for a good idea and hard work?"*
>
> Notice I gave you *three* answers, not one, and said each holds truth. That's deliberate — this is exactly the kind of question where I refuse to let you have a comfortable single story. Here's the honest picture. Risk is real (Answer 1). Organising is real work (Answer 2). *And* it's also true that employees typically produce more value than their wages, or no employer would hire them — a firm hires you precisely because you'll bring in **more** than you cost; the gap is the whole point of the job offer (Answer 3). Whether you call that gap a "fair reward for risk and organisation" or "surplus extracted from labour" is not a fact the market can settle — it's a *value judgement about who deserves what*, and it sits at the centre of every left-right argument about tax, wages, and unions. My job isn't to pick your side. It's to make sure you can never again hear the word "profit" as a simple, innocent, apolitical number. *Cui prodest?* — Lesson 1 — never stops applying.

## Competition: the engine that is also a whip 👑

Now add the second player: the restaurant across the street. Competition is usually sold to you as pure blessing — it disciplines lazy firms, lowers prices, spurs innovation. All true. But watch what it *does* to Maria, because Varoufakis wants you to feel its double face.

If her rival finds a way to make the same meal for less — a cheaper supplier, a faster oven, one fewer waiter — he can undercut her price and steal her customers. To survive, Maria must match him: cut her own costs, squeeze more output from the same wages, adopt the new oven, work everyone harder. And the moment she catches up, he innovates again. **Nobody is allowed to stop.**

This is the **Red Queen's race**, from *Alice Through the Looking-Glass*: *"it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place."* Competition forces every firm onto a treadmill of relentless cost-cutting and productivity-raising *just to survive*, let alone get ahead. This is capitalism's genuine miracle — the reason it has produced more technological change than every previous system combined. It is *also* the source of its cruelties: the ceaseless pressure to cut wages, speed up work, and discard workers and whole towns the moment they become a competitive liability. **The same whip that drives innovation drives the race to the bottom.** Both faces are one face.

Notice, too, where this points: the biggest, most squeezable cost for most firms is **wages**. So competition puts permanent downward pressure on what workers are paid — which will matter enormously when we ask, in Lesson 10, why the economy periodically crashes. (Teaser: if every firm successfully cuts its workers' wages, who is left with money to buy the restaurants' meals?)

## The gamble needs a time machine: enter debt

One last thread, tying Part II back to Part I. Remember Maria must pay *now* and earn *later*. But what if she doesn't have the thousands of euros up front? Almost no entrepreneur does. So she borrows — a bank loan, investors, credit from suppliers.

Look what that means: **the gamble across time is financed by debt** — by a claim on the future (Lesson 4) used to pay for production in the present. Capitalism doesn't just *tolerate* debt; it *runs on* it. The reversal of time (cost-before-revenue) makes borrowing not a vice but a structural necessity. This is why banks aren't a sideshow to capitalism but its central nervous system — the "time machines" we'll dissect in Lesson 9. Hold the connection: **profit is a bet across time; debt is what lets you place the bet.**

## 📉 Method Sidebar S1 — How to read a supply-and-demand diagram (and what it hides)

*You already half-know this diagram. Let me give it to you properly — and then red-pill it.*

**What it says.** Draw price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal. The **demand curve** slopes *down*: the cheaper a thing is, the more people want. The **supply curve** slopes *up*: the higher the price, the more sellers will produce. They cross at one point — the **equilibrium** — where the quantity people want to buy exactly equals the quantity sellers want to sell. That crossing gives "the market price." If price is too high, unsold surplus pushes it down; too low, shortages push it up. Elegant, self-correcting, almost beautiful.

**What it's genuinely good for.** Thinking about *one* market in isolation, and about *direction*: a bad harvest shifts supply left → price rises; a fashion boom shifts demand right → price rises. As a first-pass tool for "which way will this price move?", it's honestly useful. Keep it.

**Now, what it hides** — the four things the neat lines quietly assume away:

1. **The distribution of income is frozen and invisible.** The demand curve treats "how much people want" as if it were pure desire — but demand is *desire backed by money*. A billionaire's whim outweighs a poor family's need, because demand counts euros, not urgency. The diagram bakes in the existing distribution of wealth and then presents the result as neutral "what the market wants." *Change who has the money and every curve moves* — but the diagram never shows this.
2. **Power vanishes.** The model assumes many small buyers and sellers, none able to set prices — "perfect competition." Reality is Maria versus a landlord who can dictate the rent, workers versus an employer who sets the wage, all of us versus a handful of giant platforms. The diagram's tidy crossing erases the *bargaining power* that actually decides who gets what.
3. **Ceteris paribus — "all else held equal" — but it never is.** To draw two clean curves you must freeze everything else (tastes, incomes, other prices, expectations). Move one and the whole picture shifts. The diagram shows a still photograph and lets you mistake it for the film. *(We'll make this its own sidebar, S3, at Lesson 10.)*
4. **It treats labour and money like apples.** Draw "the labour market" with these curves and you get a tidy wage where supply meets demand — which quietly implies that unemployment is just workers "choosing" not to work at the going wage. But labour and money are the **fictitious, misbehaving commodities of Lesson 5**; their markets *don't* clear like apple markets, which is the whole reason mass unemployment and financial crashes exist. Applying S1 to them is the single most consequential error in mainstream economics.

> 💬 **Xenia interrupts:** *"But the diagram seems so obviously true — prices really do rise when things are scarce. Are you saying it's wrong?"*
>
> Not wrong — *partial*, and dangerous when mistaken for the whole. It's a genuinely good tool for the question *"which way will this one price move?"* It becomes an ideological weapon when it's used to answer *"is the outcome fair, and could it be otherwise?"* — because it has quietly assumed the existing distribution of money and power into its very axes, then hands you the answer back as if nature drew the lines. Use it like a compass: excellent for direction, useless for telling you whether you should be going that way at all.

## Key terms (say them back to yourself)

| Term | In one honest sentence |
|------|------------------------|
| **Profit** | Revenue minus costs — the gap; a *contested* quantity, variously explained as reward for risk, for organising, or surplus drawn from labour. |
| **The reversal of time** | Capitalism's core structure: costs are paid *now*, revenue arrives *later* (if at all), so production is a bet placed in the dark. |
| **The entrepreneur's gamble** | Committing real resources to an uncertain future; profit is the prize and ruin the penalty. |
| **Competition (the double face)** | The whip that drives innovation *and* the race to the bottom (wage-cutting, speed-up) — one mechanism, two faces. |
| **The Red Queen's race** | Competition's treadmill: firms must keep cutting costs and innovating just to stay in place. |
| **Supply & demand (S1)** | A useful compass for *which way a single price moves* — that hides income distribution, power, ceteris-paribus, and the misbehaving commodities. |

## The takeaway

Profit is not a simple reward and not a simple theft — it is the **gap between revenue and cost**, opened up by capitalism's defining structure: **you pay now and earn later, betting real resources on a future that may never arrive.** Where the money in that gap *comes from* — risk, organisation, or surplus drawn from labour — is a value judgement the market cannot settle, and pretending it can is the oldest trick in the book (*cui prodest?*). **Competition** is the engine that makes firms run this gamble ever harder — the same whip that powers capitalism's miraculous innovation and its race to the bottom on wages. And because you must spend before you earn, the whole system **runs on debt** — the bet is placed with borrowed future money, which is why banks sit at its centre. The tidy supply-and-demand diagram (S1) is a fine compass for *where a price is heading* and a terrible guide to *whether the outcome is fair* — because it has hidden the distribution of money and power inside its own axes.
