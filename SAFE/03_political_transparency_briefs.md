## C.2 What PSD actually said (Romanian original + translation)

The 19 May press release (carried verbatim by Agerpres, Jurnalul and DailyBusiness) accuses the *dismissed* Bolojan government — not "the Government" generically — of having facilitated:

> "**traficul de influență scandalos realizat în biroul premierului demis, Ilie Bolojan**" — în legătură cu "**o înțelegere secretă între Guvernul României și un furnizor privat german de echipamente militare**".

Translation: *the scandalous trafficking of influence carried out in the office of dismissed Prime Minister Ilie Bolojan*, in connection with *a secret agreement between the Romanian Government and a private German supplier of military equipment*.

The core complaint about the law firm:

> "**a lucrat pentru ambele părți aflate în negociere: pe de o parte, a oferit consultanță Guvernului României, iar pe de altă parte, a avut calitatea de reprezentant legal al grupului german**".

Translation: *[the firm] worked for both parties in the negotiation: on one hand it advised the Romanian Government, on the other it was the legal representative of the German group [in other matters]*.

The named individuals — **Băcanu** and **Oana Gheorghiu** — were added in later PSD briefings (Jurnalul, 20 May 2026), as was the second-order accusation that an honorary adviser to a Vice-PM was *paid by the firm advising the State on a contract that may benefit the firm's other client (the German supplier)*.

## C.3 Which German supplier is implicated?

PSD's press release never names a company. The German supplier overwhelmingly *implied* — by all triangulation against the contract record — is **Rheinmetall AG**, which is the prime in roughly €4.6–5.7 bn of the €9.6 bn MApN tranche (MLI Lynx KF41, Skynex, Skyranger 35, OPV, diver vessels, Millennium CIWS, 35 mm AHEAD ammunition, Rheinmetall Victoria propellant plant — see ledger). CMS Cameron McKenna's London partnership has acted for Rheinmetall AG in prior transactions (e.g., the 2021 Rheinmetall–4iG deal in Hungary; Marketscreener). PSD's complaint is therefore *legally coherent* even though it stops short of naming the firm.

## C.6 Strongest reading of the accusation

The strongest version of the PSD case has three legs and does not require any of them to be illegal:

1. **Process opacity**: The Chancellery (not MApN, not Finance) was the de facto controller of negotiations. Grindeanu's own admission — "I found out [about the package] on Monday when I went to Cotroceni" — confirms that *the Chamber-of-Deputies presidency and Senate leadership were not in the loop until the file was largely shaped*. The 14-hour window between distribution of the 15-contract package and the parliamentary committee vote (28 April) is consistent with this critique.
2. **Adviser-side conflict**: A government adviser was on the payroll of a law firm being paid to advise the same government. Whether or not that firm *also* represented Rheinmetall on this specific transaction, **it has represented Rheinmetall AG in past transactions**. Even by Romanian conflict-of-interest law (Law 161/2003) — which is permissive by EU standards — the appearance is sufficient to require disclosure and recusal, neither of which is documented.
3. **Outcome alignment**: The end-state allocation — €4.66–5.69 bn (roughly 50–60% of MApN's SAFE tranche) to a single German prime, awarded through "negotiation without prior publication" under OUG 62/2025 art. 4(5) — is consistent with a process tilted toward an incumbent. This is the *circumstantial* leg; on its own, it proves nothing.

## C.7 Strongest counter-reading

1. **Caveat: PSD has the strongest possible political motive.** PSD has just lost government, has just amended-out 12 articles of the rival's OUG, and is actively triggering CCR review. Its messaging is structured for political consumption.
2. **CMS engagement values are small.** ≈ €60,000 over four months is a fraction of normal magic-circle hourly rates and far below what would be needed to "buy" a €5 bn contract direction. The Government's competing-quotes claim (four firms; CMS lowest) is consistent with that magnitude.
3. **CMS's role was narrow.** Per the Government's clarification: "strictly limited to transposing into contracts and ensuring legal conformity of industrial cooperation agreements, not commercial negotiations." If true and verifiable (the engagement letter has not been published), the conflict-of-interest exposure is *contractual hygiene*, not deal-making.
4. **The structural choice to go to Rheinmetall predates this engagement.** Rheinmetall's industrial footprint in Romania (Automecanica Mediaș since 2023; Pirochim/Victoria JV signed 3 Nov 2025) was already in place. CMS could not have caused the Lynx decision.
5. **No DNA file, no Court of Accounts file, no whistleblower with documents.** The accusation lives in press releases and CCR notifications, not in a prosecutorial record.

## C.8 Open questions

- Was CMS's engagement put through SEAP/SICAP, and is the procurement notice retrievable?
- Did CMS hold a current mandate from Rheinmetall AG (not just past mandates) during the April 2026 engagement?
- What is in the CMS engagement letter — specifically, does it carve out industrial-cooperation drafting for the Lynx programme?
- Will the CCR's ruling on either of the two notifications (Grindeanu's and the Ombudsman's) be issued before 31 May 2026 — i.e., before contract signature?
- Has the parliamentary defence committee requested the engagement letter? No record yet.

## C.9 Sidebar — Miruță's *other* DNA referral (the Pop Industry case)

Not strictly part of the SAFE-influence-peddling story, but materially relevant to assessing the **credibility** of Miruță's procurement-reform posture (which features in Thread D as the price-hike whistleblower) is his 23 March 2026 DNA referral on **Pop Industry SRL**, a company controlled by the family of Slatina businessman Dumitru Popescu (G4Media, defapt.ro, Republik News, Puterea, AS in Gorj, Normedia).

- **Allegation**: Pop Industry won ~€66 m of cumulative MApN / IGSU / Romanian Police / State Reserves Administration contracts with technical specifications **set six times above the operational standard** (e.g., tents specced at 18,000 mm water column versus the 3,000 mm normally adequate), so only Pop Industry could qualify.
- **Defective delivery**: Military containers worth €7.6 m delivered to Făgăraș and Smârdan with substituted brands, missing AC units, missing windows, technical diagrams not matching offer — yet reception committees signed off.
- **Minister's action**: Referred to DNA (anti-corruption) *and* the ministry's own Directorate for Prevention and Investigation of Corruption.
- **USR framing** (the minister's party): the irregularities are *evidence* that the SAFE €17 bn programme requires the new platform-based regime to prevent recurrence — "Romanian soldiers deserve real equipment, not leftovers from connected firms."

The Pop Industry case is **outside SAFE** (the contracts predate SAFE flows) but establishes Miruță's *track record of using DNA referral on procurement issues*. The minister has not made an analogous DNA referral on any SAFE programme — including Rheinmetall. This is meaningful: it suggests either (a) the SAFE concerns are real but do not rise to a criminal threshold in his view, or (b) the political cost of referring a sitting prime contractor mid-negotiation is too high to bear. Both interpretations are consistent with his contemporaneous *public* criticism without prosecutorial follow-through.

# THREAD D — Minister Radu Miruță's "30% price hike at signature" complaint

## D.1 The original statement

**Venue & date:** The Economist Romania Government Roundtable 2026, held in Bucharest on **31 March 2026** (article published 1 April 2026 by Defense Romania; corroborated by Mediafax, FollowTheMoney, Observator News, Realitatea).

**Exact wording (Romanian original — Defense Romania transcript):**

> "Am început să discutăm, iar acesta este un anunţ în premieră, în jurul proiectelor SAFE. Unii producători de muniţie au anunţat unele preţuri. Acum, când urmează să semnăm contractele, au revenit către noi cu o majorare de 30% a acestor preţuri iniţiale."

Translation: *We began discussing — and this is an announcement in advance — around the SAFE projects. Some ammunition manufacturers had announced certain prices. Now, when we are about to sign the contracts, they have come back to us with a 30% increase on those initial prices.*

A second emblematic Miruță quote, dated April 2026 (Defence Industry Europe summary, FollowTheMoney transcript):

> "When we started discussions around SAFE, there were some private ammunition producers who announced a price. Now, when we are about to sign the contracts, they are coming back to us with about 30% more. The ministry will not give in to the pressure of time. The private sector cannot push us to accept higher prices just because we have to sign the contract by the end of May 2026."

A third Miruță statement, made later (mid-May 2026, Defense Romania transcript):

> "**Da, văd riscul să se piardă contracte în SAFE dacă se tergiversează.**" — *Yes, I see the risk that contracts may be lost in SAFE if there is delay.*

He framed the cost of the inflated offer in arithmetic terms:

> "Acela a fost un moment definitoriu pentru că **SAFE înseamnă un împrumut cu dobândă de 3,5%, 10 ani perioadă de grație** ... [dar] dacă tot plătim 30% în plus, nu mai are sens împrumutul ieftin."

Translation: *That was a defining moment because SAFE means a loan at 3.5% interest, with a 10-year grace period ... but if we still pay 30% more, the cheap loan loses its point.*

## D.2 Which programmes and which suppliers?

| Programme | Press allegations on price hike | Final budget figure |
|-----------|-------------------------------|--------------------|
| **MLI Lynx KF41** (232–298 IFVs) | Original press estimate €2.98 bn; Defence Industry Europe reports Rheinmetall offer >30% above initial expectations (~€1 bn add) | Signed at ≈ €3.337 bn for the 232 SAFE-funded units (≈+12%). Miruță attributes the rise to *Spike anti-tank missiles added to spec*, not pure mark-up; he also says Romania's €11.2 m/unit price is below the €13.7 m average Rheinmetall charges others. |
| **OPV** (2 patrol vessels) | €700 m → €836 m (≈+20%) | €836 m; tied to Mangalia shipyard rescue |
| **Diver intervention vessels** (2) | €57 m → €84 m (≈+47%) | €84 m |
| **35 mm ammunition** (Skynex / Skyranger / AHEAD) | Generically referenced by Miruță as "private ammunition producers"; Rheinmetall Waffe Munition is the prime | €23.15 m (CRAM) + €393.28 m (AHEAD) |

The minister's headline "30%" figure is best understood as **a portfolio-level price drift across multiple programmes** — not a single 30% bump on a single contract. It maps cleanly onto Rheinmetall lots; it does **not** map onto Airbus, Diehl, MBDA, Thales, Kongsberg, WB Group, IDV, or GDELS, all of which appear in the ledger without a comparable price-hike controversy.

## D.3 Rheinmetall's response

**No on-record statement from Rheinmetall** addressing Miruță's 30% accusation has surfaced in this research. Defense Industry Europe, the most-cited trade outlet on this dispute, runs Miruță's allegations without a paired Rheinmetall comment. Rheinmetall's only documented public posture in the May 2026 window is:

- the **3 November 2025 Pirochim Victoria JV announcement** (positive PR);
- press-source-only claims (Comisarul.ro, defapt.ro, Bursa) that Rheinmetall as of **11 May 2026** sought to *renegotiate downward* its local-content commitment (from ~60% to ~40%) before signature — which would be a different kind of concession demand than a price hike per se.

Rheinmetall has not denied the price-hike framing. It has not confirmed it either. The trade press appears to be relying entirely on Romanian government sources for the alleged 30%.

## D.4 What MApN says it did about it

Per MApN's official communication of 11 May 2026 (published on Juridice.ro 12 May):

- Evaluation committees operate "**independent and impartial**" of supplier pressure.
- The ministry **"will not sign procurement contracts in conditions disadvantageous to the institution**, considering the approved documents of the award procedure and the principle of efficient use of public funds."
- Award procedures applied are *negotiation without prior publication of a participation notice*, per OUG 62/2025 and OUG 114/2011 art. 56.

Miruță has separately said his strategy was to set price ceilings ≈30% below the suppliers' inflated offers and force suppliers to either accept or walk (Defense Romania mid-May report).

Romania, which is buying mostly *from* foreign primes, sits at the wrong end of that asymmetry.

## D.6 Strongest reading of Miruță's complaint

1. **Specific and time-stamped.** Unlike PSD's generic "influence peddling" framing, Miruță names a phenomenon (price drift at the last hour), a magnitude (30%), and points implicitly at the largest supplier (Rheinmetall) without sliding into political abstraction.
2. **The deadline-pressure incentive structure is real.** The 31 May 2026 hard EU deadline gives any incumbent supplier maximal leverage. Miruță is naming a structural problem of SAFE's design as much as of Romania's negotiation.
3. **The savings claim is verifiable.** Government reporting (Gandul) says PM Bolojan negotiated the Rheinmetall package down from ≈€6.51 bn to ≈€5.69 bn — i.e., ≈ €820 m saved. The directionality of that number supports Miruță's framing that *initial offers were inflated and were rolled back under pressure*.
4. **Miruță is putting the marker down before signature.** This matters: if there is a future audit dispute, the minister's contemporaneous 31 March statement establishes that he flagged the issue prospectively, not retrospectively.

## D.7 Strongest counter-reading

1. **The 30% figure is single-source.** It comes from one minister, in one speech, and has not been validated by a published comparison of *original-RFI* prices versus *signed* prices on a contract-by-contract basis.
2. **Add-on scope explains some of it.** Miruță himself concedes the Lynx unit price rose because Spike ATGM was added to spec. Spec creep is not the same as supplier price-gouging — although the line between them in negotiations without prior publication is by construction fuzzy.
3. **Currency and inflation drift over 2024–2026 partially mask the picture.** Rheinmetall's own input costs (steel, electronics, propellants) rose materially over the same window. Some portion of a 30% nominal move reflects honest cost-pass-through.
4. **Rheinmetall's silence is *not* an admission.** German large defence primes rarely comment on live negotiations; the burden of proof remains with the accuser.
5. **The "I will not sign disadvantageous contracts" posture is partly theatre.** Given MApN's own incentive to sign before 31 May (otherwise lose the loan tranche), the credible threat to walk is small. Suppliers know this. Miruță is bluffing partially.

## D.9 Open questions

- What is the *contract-by-contract* delta between RFI price and signed price? Without this, the 30% claim cannot be tested.
- Does the OPV programme price rise of 20% reflect a *legitimate* Mangalia-shipyard rescue premium (industrial policy) or supplier opportunism?

## D.10 Sidebar — the OPV price comparison with Bulgaria

A concrete data-point on Romanian SAFE price-discipline is the **OPV (Offshore Patrol Vessel) programme**, where Warsight's analysis (citing Monitorul Apărării și Securității and supplier records) reports that:

- Romania is paying **€836 m for 2 OPV 90 vessels** (≈ €418 m per ship).
- Bulgaria recently contracted **similar-class OPVs from Rheinmetall/Lürssen at materially lower per-ship price** — the Warsight analyst characterises the Romanian unit-price as carrying a ~40% premium for nominally the same hull.
- The premium is partly explained by **added subsystems** (Romania's spec includes fire-control radar, CBRN detection, hull sonar, full CMS, CIWS, anti-torpedo countermeasures, RAM point-defence, torpedo launcher — see Naval News' enumeration of what Damen's rejected €457 m proposal *omitted*).
- It is partly explained by the **Mangalia shipyard rescue surcharge**: the contract pays a Romanian builder to acquire know-how that exists already in German/Dutch shipyards.

This is the cleanest example of a *transparent* premium-for-industrial-policy in SAFE: the higher price is the deliberate price of saving 3,000–5,000 Romanian jobs. Whether that price is *worth* paying is a defensible question; that the higher price *exists* is documented and was acknowledged by Miruță when justifying the rejection of Damen.

## D.11 Sidebar — the Lynx counter-comparison (Romania pays *less* than peers)

Where the OPV story makes Romania look like it overpaid, the **Lynx unit-price story** runs the opposite direction. Miruță explicitly stated:

- Romania's €11.2 m per KF41 Lynx unit is **below the €13.7 m average Rheinmetall charges other nations** (HotNews, Miruță statement on programme).
- Romania becomes the **world's largest KF41 operator** at this volume — surpassing Hungary and Ukraine. Volume should buy discount.
- The €330 m rise from earlier estimates reflects the addition of **Spike anti-tank missiles** to spec, not pure price gouging.

This makes the "30% price hike" story more nuanced than the headline framing: in the *largest* SAFE programme by value (Lynx, ≈€3.34 bn), Romania appears to have *negotiated reasonably well* — at the cost of accepting a single-source process with no competitor on the table. The price discipline came from Bolojan's direct intervention (the ≈€820 m claimed Rheinmetall-package reduction), not from competitive tension.

# THREAD E — Law 4/2026 transparency carve-outs

## E.1 The legal stack — what each layer does

Romania's SAFE governance is built on **four overlapping normative layers**, not one law. Understanding the transparency record requires unpacking the stack:

1. **EU Regulation 2025/1106** of 27 May 2025 — the SAFE Regulation. Directly applicable. Defines eligibility, the 65% EU-content rule, SDP-country participation, and reporting obligations.
2. **OUG 114/2011, approved by Law 195/2012** — Romania's *pre-existing* defence and security procurement framework, transposing EU Directive 2009/81/EC. This already permits restricted procedures, negotiation, and exclusion of open tender for classified items.
3. **OUG 62/2025**, adopted 20 November 2025 — the *SAFE-specific* implementation OUG. Adds an explicit power to use **negotiation without prior publication of a participation notice** for SAFE projects (essentially direct award after CSAT pre-approval of the operator), on grounds of "high complexity" and "optimal cost-efficiency."
4. **Law 4/2026** (published 9 January 2026 in Monitorul Oficial Part I, no. 10) — approves OUG 62/2025 with one completion (new art. 4 para. 5, extending the urgency presumption to *highway infrastructure projects* financed or financeable from SAFE). This is the headline statute but materially adds little; it is mostly a parliamentary ratification.
5. **OUG 21/2026** — further amendments to OUG 62/2025 (less politically contentious; mostly procedural fixes).
6. **OUG 38/2026**, adopted 4 May 2026 by the *no-confidence-defeated* Bolojan government — broadens the SAFE legal package (strategic-asset protection, insolvency carve-outs, expropriation procedures). **This is the politically contested one.** It is the subject of the Ombudsman's and Grindeanu's CCR notifications.

## E.2 Derogations from Law 98/2016 (the general public-procurement law)

Several derogations are introduced. Each is a *narrowing of transparency* relative to the default civilian procurement regime:

| Derogation | Source | What changes |
|-----------|---------|--------------|
| **No prior publication of participation notice** | OUG 62/2025 art. 4 (as modified) | The contracting authority (MApN, MAI, SRI) sends invitations only to economic operators **pre-approved by CSAT**. There is no public RFP. |
| **Urgency presumption (no case-by-case justification)** | OUG 62/2025; Law 4/2026 art. 4(5) | The "urgency" required by EU Directive 2009/81/EC art. 28(c) is *presumed* by statute. No file-level justification of urgency must be filed in each case. Juridice.ro analysis flags ambiguity over whether this presumption is absolute or rebuttable. |
| **Exemption from standard offset rules** | OUG 62/2025 (CMS Law analysis) | Romania's general defence-procurement offset rules (Law 232/2016, the so-called "compensare industrială" regime) do **not** apply to SAFE contracts; industrial cooperation is negotiated bilaterally on the platform. |
| **Open tender with reduced timelines (for highway infrastructure)** | Law 4/2026 added art. 4(5) | "Licitație deschisă cu reducerea termenelor" — i.e., open tender but with statutory deadline shortening — for SAFE-financed highway projects (A7 Pașcani–Suceava–Siret, A8 Mocta–Iași–Ungheni). Tender is still public but faster. |
| **Approval of contracts > €100 m by Parliament becomes a *prior approval*, not an open hearing** | OUG 114/2011 art. 56; OUG 62/2025 | Standard Romanian rule (Law 195/2012) requires parliamentary prior approval over €100 m. SAFE keeps this requirement but conducts it through the *joint defence committees*, not plenary, and with classified annexes. |
| **Personnel and budget exceptions to austerity ordinances for MApN DGArm** | OUG 38/2026 (and earlier) | Exempts up to 10 DGArm positions from hiring freezes; exempts SAFE-related travel and inter-ministerial approval rules for procurements >€10 m. (Fanatik.ro, legislative materials.) |
| **Strategic-asset protection / insolvency provisions** | OUG 38/2026 (constitutionally contested) | State can acquire functional assets in private ownership under specified memorandum conditions; expropriation procedures modernised under Law 255/2010. |

## E.3 What is classified by default?

Per OUG 114/2011 art. 4 and standing CSAT classification practice:

- **Operational requirements** (the technical specifications that drive the choice of supplier) are by default classified at minimum "Strict Secret de Serviciu", often "Secret".
- **CSAT decisions** approving specific procurement procedures and operators are **classified**, with the title and date sometimes declassified for public communication but the body usually not.
- **Award documentation** (the *documentation de atribuire*) is treated as classified for SAFE programmes, justified under OUG 114/2011's national-security carve-out from Law 98/2016.
- **The list of CSAT-pre-approved operators** for each programme is **not published**; press identifies them by inference from MApN press releases and subsequent contract announcements.
- **Bidder evaluation reports** — to the extent they exist for "negotiation without prior publication" procedures (Q Magazine argues they functionally do not, because there is only one bidder per programme) — are classified.

Per CMS Law's published explainer, the SAFE legal framework "introduces special rules and exemptions applicable to procurement award procedures for projects funded through SAFE funds, departing from the rules generally applicable to other defence-related projects."

## E.5 CSAT's role under the law

The Supreme Defence Council of the Country (Consiliul Suprem de Apărare a Țării, CSAT, chaired by the President) has under OUG 62/2025 a *gating* role:

- It **approves the inter-institutional working group's recommended economic operators** for each SAFE programme (the so-called "lista de operatori economici aprobați CSAT").
- This approval *replaces* the public RFP. Once an operator is on the list, the contracting authority (MApN's DGArm under Brigadier General Pleșa, or MAI's procurement bodies, or SRI) issues a participation invitation only to that operator.
- CSAT also approves the **list of programmes** itself before they go to Parliament for the >€100 m prior approval.

This gives the **Presidency** (via the CSAT chair, currently President Nicușor Dan) a constitutive role in shaping who can win SAFE contracts. The President's authority here is *de facto*, exercised through CSAT majority. The 2025–2026 sequence — CSAT approves operators in closed session, Parliament committee approves package, MApN sends invitations — produces a chain where no single body can be held publicly accountable for the choice of supplier, because the *operative* decision is taken behind the CSAT classification screen.

## E.6 Comparison with pre-SAFE Romanian defence-procurement transparency

| Feature | Pre-SAFE regime (OUG 114/2011 + Law 195/2012) | SAFE regime (OUG 62/2025 + Law 4/2026 + OUG 38/2026) |
|---------|----------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| Open tender available | Yes (although often waived under defence carve-out) | Effectively no for SAFE procurements |
| Case-by-case justification of urgency | Required | **Presumed by statute** |
| Standard offset rules (Law 232/2016) | Apply | **Exempted** |
| Parliamentary prior approval > €100 m | Plenary involvement available | Committee-only (defence committees joint), with reduced lead time |
| Court of Accounts ex-post audit | Routine | Routine (but no published SAFE-specific audit as of 22 May 2026) |
| Time pressure | Programme-dependent | **Hard 31 May 2026 EU deadline drives all timing** |

The net effect is: Romania has *narrowed transparency* relative to its already-narrowed defence regime, in exchange for speed and EU loan access. This is consistent with the SAFE Regulation's own pressure for fast disbursement, but the *Romanian-specific* choices (CSAT operator pre-approval, classified award documentation, no published evaluation report) are decisions Bucharest made, not requirements imposed by Brussels.

## E.7 Comparison with Polish and Czech equivalents

**Poland.** Poland's SAFE implementation works through the Polish Armaments Group (PGZ), HSW, Rosomak SA and other state-owned primes, which are *themselves* the negotiating parties. The transparency loss is similar in form (limited public tendering), but the *economic* loss is different: Polish funds stay in Polish industry (89% per MoND), so domestic political support is overwhelming. The PiS-vs-PO political tension over SAFE in Poland in 2026 was less about transparency than about presidential signature on the loan agreement (Nawrocki threatened to withhold).

**Czechia.** Czechia's procurement runs through the Ministry of Defence's National Electronic Tool (NEN), which is statutorily transparent — all contract data are published. CSG (Czechoslovak Group) and other private primes are part of a more market-oriented system; CSG itself runs a Transparency-International-affiliated compliance program. The *baseline* transparency is markedly better than Romania's, but Czechia is a smaller SAFE recipient (allocation not yet itemised in this brief's sources) so it is not a direct like-for-like comparison.

**Italy.** Italy's €14.9 bn SAFE allocation is structured around Leonardo and Fincantieri as national champions. Procurement is conducted under the Italian defence-procurement law (Codice degli appalti pubblici, with defence-specific carve-outs largely parallel to OUG 114/2011's Romanian transposition of EU Dir 2009/81/EC). Transparency International's 2021 *Defence Industry Influence in Italy* report rated Italy as having significant industry influence on policymaking — i.e., the transparency baseline is mid-tier, not best-in-class. Italy is *closer* to Romania structurally than Poland is, but with two crucial differences: (a) Italy is *also a producer* of major SAFE-eligible systems (so 65% EU-content rules favour its industry), and (b) Italy has not experienced anything like Romania's coalition crisis around SAFE. There has been no Italian equivalent of the PSD/Grindeanu CCR notification, no Italian Miruță-style "30%" complaint, and no Italian OUG 38/2026 caretaker-government constitutional dispute. Italy's politics are unusually placid on SAFE because Leonardo and Fincantieri are the visible beneficiaries.

**Pattern across the three peers.** The cross-cutting observation is that **transparency intensity correlates inversely with foreign-prime exposure**:

- Poland (low exposure, ~11% foreign content): low *need* for transparency, very high political support, weak external scrutiny.
- Czechia (mixed exposure, NEN platform): high *baseline* transparency by design, less political controversy.
- Italy (low foreign exposure via Leonardo/Fincantieri primacy): moderate transparency, very low political heat.
- Romania (high foreign exposure, ~50–63% to German firms): moderate-to-low transparency by design, very high political heat.

Romania's transparency record looks worst-of-peers in part because **transparency is needed most where political dispute is highest, and political dispute is highest where the foreign-leakage problem is largest**. Romania sits at the worst point on both axes simultaneously.

## E.9 Strongest reading (transparency-deficit case)

- The SAFE legal stack creates a *parallel* procurement regime in which the foundational competitive-tender principle of Law 98/2016 is suspended.
- The chain "CSAT pre-approves operators (classified) → contracting authority sends invitation only to that operator (no public notice) → committee approves package on tight timeline (closed substantive discussion) → contract signed (terms classified) → 3-month report (format unspecified)" is one in which the only public moment is the headline figure.
- The **same official (General Pleșa)** sits on the working group proposing operators, designates the evaluation committee, and signs the contracts. Separation of functions has functionally collapsed.
- Adviser-of-Vice-PM holds a position in the law firm advising the State on industrial-cooperation contracts that benefit a single dominant supplier. No formal disclosure record is found.

## E.10 Strongest counter-reading (defence-of-the-regime case)

- OUG 114/2011 already allowed negotiation without prior publication on national-security grounds. SAFE did not invent the carve-out; it *systematised* its use.
- EU Directive 2009/81/EC explicitly permits Member States to handle defence procurement under national-security exceptions to the standard regime. Romania's regime is **not unique in Europe**.
- The 65% EU-content rule under SAFE pushes Romania toward fewer suppliers (the qualifying primes), structurally limiting how competitive any process could be. The "negotiation without prior publication" choice is partly a consequence of a small qualifying universe, not a Romanian opacity preference.
- Parliamentary prior approval is preserved, the >€100 m threshold is preserved, the CSAT screen is in line with prior practice for sensitive procurements, and the Court of Accounts retains *ex-post* audit jurisdiction.
- Pauliuc's amendments (mandatory 50% domestic investment, 3-month post-signature reports) add *new* oversight not present in the prior regime.

## E.11 Open questions

- Will the CCR uphold OUG 38/2026? If yes, the package consolidates; if no, the strategic-asset and insolvency carve-outs vanish and Rheinmetall's Mangalia path narrows.
- Will the Court of Accounts open a *concomitent* audit on SAFE in 2026, or only ex-post in 2027? No indication yet.
- Will the 3-month post-signature compliance reports be public, partly public, or classified?
- What does the CSAT-approved operator list look like programme-by-programme, and will any version (even redacted) be declassified?

## E.12 The "Cooperation Agreement" (Acordul de Cooperare) — a new enforcement instrument

A specific element of OUG 21/2026 (which further amends OUG 62/2025) deserves separate emphasis because it is the *only* mechanism in the entire legal stack that gives Romania *contractual* (not merely political) leverage over the offset commitments.

The Cooperation Agreement (*acord de cooperare*) is a **new statutory instrument** that:

- **Sits alongside** the principal procurement contract (the supply contract proper);
- **Codifies industrial-cooperation obligations** of the foreign prime — what will be produced in Romania, by which Romanian companies, in what timelines, at what value share;
- **Conditions product reception** (the technical-acceptance milestone that triggers payment) **on certified fulfilment of those obligations**.

In plain language: the prime supplier cannot get paid for delivering a Lynx vehicle unless it can also demonstrate that the Romanian local-content milestone for that delivery has been hit.

This is a meaningful design choice. Under the prior regime (Law 232/2016 industrial offset rules, *exempted* by Law 4/2026 for SAFE), offset commitments were treated as parallel obligations whose breach was punished by performance bonds — slow, contested, often unenforced. The Cooperation Agreement *integrates* the offset into the principal contract's payment trigger.

**Caveats and unknowns:**

- The exact text of the standard Cooperation Agreement template has not been published.
- It is not clear whether the certification of "obligation fulfilled" is conducted by an independent body, by MApN's DGArm (which faces the General Pleșa conflict-of-functions critique above), or by a CSAT-designated entity.
- It is not clear whether the public will see the Cooperation Agreements at all — or whether they will be classified annexes to the supply contracts.
- The Cooperation Agreement was introduced *after* the Bolojan-Papperger 3 November 2025 MoU and *after* most of the operator pre-approvals — so for some early-stage programmes it is a retrofit, not a forward-engineered control.

If the Cooperation Agreement is implemented as designed, and if the certification mechanism is rigorous, it is the **single most-promising element** of the SAFE legal stack from a Romanian industrial-interest perspective. If it is implemented as a paper exercise — performance verified by the same DGArm that approved the contract — it becomes another classification-shielded check-box.

## E.13 The pre-SAFE transparency baseline — Balkan Insight "Fog of War"

For context on whether SAFE *worsens* an already-bad baseline or merely *reproduces* it under a new label, the February 2025 Balkan Insight BIRN investigation "Fog of War" is the indispensable reference.

That investigation, conducted *before* SAFE flows began, documented:

- Local military units (e.g., the **53rd Commando Battalion at Bacău**) awarding contracts to barely-formed companies (Area Best Construct SRL — incorporated 2022, 4 employees), at unit prices well above market rates (€294 per bed versus market 433–584 lei).
- **Deveselu Barracks** awarding 179,175 lei to Helen M Construct SRL three months after its incorporation in July 2023.
- A **Dâmbovița unit** awarding 739,500 lei to Eurotest Solutions SRL, associated with an individual already under DNA corruption investigation.
- A company (Clemans SRL) securing **21 separate contracts** while under tax-evasion investigation for ~€500,000 in damages.
- Patterns of **former military personnel running private companies winning state contracts**, **military employees simultaneously working for contractor companies**, and **spouses of military officials employed by contract winners**.
- **Four cases referred to prosecutors 2022–2024** by MApN itself, involving garrisons in Focșani, Capu Midia and Craiova.

The pre-SAFE baseline was, in short, **bad**. Romania's defence-procurement transparency record before SAFE was characterised by routine *small-scale* irregularities at unit level, structural conflicts of interest, and infrequent but real prosecutorial action.

SAFE *changes the scale* (from €100,000 to €100 million procurements) but *uses the same legal carve-outs* (OUG 114/2011) that produced the Balkan Insight pattern. The good news is that SAFE's scale and EU visibility force more centralised processes — CSAT-level pre-approval, parliamentary committee votes, mandatory compliance reports. The bad news is that the same scale concentration creates fewer, larger points of failure if those centralised processes are themselves captured.

This is why Pauliuc's amendments and the Cooperation Agreement mechanism matter so much. They are *the* corrective layer added on top of a regime whose unmodified default would, by reasonable extrapolation from Balkan Insight, produce the same kinds of small-scale capture but at orders-of-magnitude larger contract size.

## F.7 Poland comparison — how an industrial offset is supposed to look

Poland's SAFE construction is **fundamentally different** from Romania's:

- **89% of Polish SAFE funds will stay in Polish industry** (Polish MoND, via Breaking Defense). 12,000 Polish companies expected to benefit.
- The structure is "buy from PGZ / HSW / Rosomak / Jelcz / PIT-RADWAR" — i.e., **Polish state-owned and Polish private primes are the direct contracting parties**, not foreign primes with local-content side-deals.
- The 11% that goes abroad is identified (United States, South Korea) and is mostly capability the Polish industry cannot deliver.

The Romanian model is the inverse: the *foreign prime* is the direct contracting party, and *local content* is negotiated as a percentage of the foreign prime's deliverable. Risk allocation differs accordingly. Poland captures industrial value by being the manufacturer. Romania captures industrial value by *contracting* the manufacturer to do part of the work locally.

The reason for the difference is straightforward — Poland's defence industry, despite its own dysfunctions, has *capacity* (Krab SPH, K2PL by license, Rosomak by license, ZSSW-30 turret). Romania's does not, in the high-end segments. The PSD/AUR critique that "the money leaves Romania" is *structurally true* of Romania's lot but does not reflect a uniquely Romanian failure of negotiation — it reflects 30 years of underinvestment in the Romanian defence-industrial base.

## F.8 Strongest reading of "the money leaves Romania"

1. **Even at 50% local content**, ~€8.3 bn of MApN's € 16.6 bn (or ~€4.7 bn of the €9.6 bn MApN tranche) flows to foreign primes' home value chains. AUR's "10 billion to Germany and France" claim is order-of-magnitude correct.
2. **Local content** ≠ Romanian industrial sovereignty. Assembling a vehicle that someone else designed, owns the IP for, and supplies the high-value components for (turrets, electronics, sensors) is a low-IP, low-margin activity. The 40% Lynx local content is by volume; by *value* it may be considerably less than 40%, because the turret alone is half the value.
3. **Mangalia rescue is presented as industrial policy** but is materially closer to a state-backed bail-out routed through a procurement contract — see Bursa.ro's April 24 piece on "MApN artificially inflates SAFE funding to cosmeticize Mangalia collapse" alleging ~€1 bn premium to cover ~€180 m of shipyard debt.
4. **Romarm SOEs other than Pirochim Victoria are barely named** in the public SAFE allocation. Carfil and Cugir are obvious candidates for €440 m of small-arms and ammo, but no contract has been announced.

## F.9 Strongest counter-reading

1. **The propellant-powder plant at Victoria is real, large, and strategically significant.** ~€535 m investment, ~700 direct jobs, sovereign capability for modular propellants the Romanian Army currently cannot produce. This is the kind of capability Romania could not afford to build alone in a 10-year horizon.
2. **Mangalia is being saved.** Whatever the premium, the alternative is the shipyard's permanent closure, loss of 3,000–5,000 skilled jobs, and the disappearance of Romania's Black Sea naval-construction capacity. SAFE is the only available financing vehicle.
3. **40% Lynx local content is more than zero**, and the Spike ATGM integration brings Israeli–Romanian secondary supply-chain potential.
4. **The Pauliuc 50%-minimum amendment is enforceable** through the 3-month reporting cycle — if compliance reporting is rigorous, the offset moves from aspirational to legally compulsory.
5. **The Jurca digital platform**, if it works as advertised, gives Romanian SMEs *visibility* into prime-contractor sub-need lists for the first time — a step-change in market access.
6. **Even Poland's 89% figure is itself the result of three decades of consistent industrial-base investment**. Romania can build toward that ratio; SAFE is not a single chance, it is the *first* chance.

## F.10 Open questions

- What is the *signed-contract* local-content percentage for each of the 21 programmes? Pauliuc's reports are the test.
- Who wins the €440 m small-arms+ammo contract? Cugir (Romarm) or H&K via Cugir JV would be a major sovereignty win.
- Will Rheinmetall actually invest the €400 m of its share in the Victoria propellant plant, or will it find ways to scale down? The 11 May 2026 reports of Rheinmetall wanting to reduce localisation thresholds suggest the latter is a live risk.
- Does the new SAFE digital platform receive meaningful Romanian-SME registration, or does it become a procurement-theatre artifact?
- Of the 21 programmes, how many ultimately have *quantified* technology-transfer commitments (vs. headline percentages)?

## F.11 The 31 December 2030 hard deadline — why the offset story is time-boxed

A point made by Warsight's analyst that does not get enough domestic emphasis: **the entire SAFE financing must be paid out by 31 December 2030**. That is the EU Regulation's hard limit. For each contract, this means *the entire deliverable must be completed and the work demonstrated by that date* — not signed, not started, *completed*.

This has direct consequences for the industrial-offset story:

- **Romania has ~4.5 years** to build the propellant plant (3-year construction window), train the Mangalia OPV labour force, ramp Automecanica Mediaș from a parts supplier to a 40%-content assembly operation, and qualify Romanian sub-tier suppliers across 21 programmes. The plant alone is a stretch.
- **If domestic production cannot be ramped fast enough**, the deliverable may be substituted by foreign-built items — with the local-content number adjusted downward in the final compliance report (the Pauliuc 3-month report mechanism) or, more likely, the local-content commitment quietly *renegotiated* during contract performance.
- **Rheinmetall's 11 May 2026 reported attempt to renegotiate localisation downward** (from ~60% to ~40%, per Comisarul.ro and defapt.ro) is best understood in this light: the prime is positioning to argue that timeline pressure makes the original commitment infeasible.
- **The Cooperation Agreement** (Thread E.12) is the only contractual remedy. Whether it works depends entirely on whether MApN insists on certifying obligation-fulfilment before payment, or accepts performance plans that get renegotiated as deadlines slip.

This 31 December 2030 deadline is also why the local-content debate is the *highest-stakes* element of the entire SAFE programme for Romania. Other elements of the deal (debt service terms, capability acquired) are largely fixed at signature. The industrial-content delivery is the only element where *post-signature behaviour* still moves the needle materially.

## F.12 Sidebar — the share-of-funds-to-Germany debate (numerical reconciliation)

Three published estimates of "how much of Romania's SAFE goes to Germany" circulate in May 2026 reporting; reconciling them is important:

| Source | German-share figure | Method |
|--------|---------------------|--------|
| Gandul ("Romania SAFE report") | ~€7 bn of €16.68 bn to Germany (≈42%); of which Rheinmetall €5.69 bn + Diehl €574 m + Quantum Systems €31 m | Sum of identified German prime contractors across MApN tranche |
| Criticarad.ro | €5.69 bn to Rheinmetall = 59.7% of MApN budget (excluding MAI/SRI, infrastructure) | Rheinmetall share of MApN-only tranche |
| Warsight / Monitorul Apărării și Securității | ~€6 bn (≈63%) of MApN's €9.53 bn could flow to German firms; ~€5 bn (≈52%) to Rheinmetall alone | German-firm share of MApN-only €9.53 bn defence tranche |
| AUR (Simion) | "€10 bn of €16 bn to Germany and France" | Political framing, includes France's ~€1 bn Airbus/Thales/MBDA share |

The numerical *consensus* — across all four sources — is that **Germany captures somewhere between 42% (whole envelope) and 63% (defence tranche only)** of the SAFE money flow, with Rheinmetall accounting for the lion's share.

This sets up the test of Research Plan Hypothesis H1 ("German primes will capture > 40% of Romania's SAFE-funded contract value by primary award; falsified if below 25%"). **H1 is comfortably confirmed by primary award** even on the most conservative measurement. The interesting follow-on question for the rest of the study (out of scope for Threads C–F) is how much of that primary-award share *flows back* to Romania via local content and the Cooperation Agreement — i.e., the net-of-offset share, which is the meaningful number for the "German bailout" thesis.

## F.13 The wider Bolojan defence — "I disturbed many"

A useful additional data-point on the political-economy reading of Threads C and F is PM Bolojan's own *post hoc* interview (Jurnalul, "Bolojan rupe tăcerea despre SAFE și Rheinmetall: «Am deranjat foarte mult»"). His framing is that PSD's attacks reflect his having "disturbed many [interests]" by pushing SAFE through. This is the standard reformer-against-vested-interests defence. It is partially credible (PSD's attack does fit a "we're losing influence" pattern) and partially deflective (it elides the structural question of whether the supplier choice was justified on capability grounds).

Bolojan's specific defence on the Băcanu / Gheorghiu / CMS triangle:

> "Nu văd nicio formă de conflict de interese. Dna Gheorghiu nu a fost implicată în negocierile pe SAFE, ea are altă zonă de competențe."

Translation: *I see no form of conflict of interest. Mrs Gheorghiu was not involved in the SAFE negotiations; she has a different area of competence.*

This is a *narrow* defence — it addresses Gheorghiu's personal involvement, not the law-firm's structural conflict, and not Băcanu's payment from CMS while serving as Gheorghiu's adviser. It does not address whether Băcanu had access to or influence over SAFE files via his proximity to Gheorghiu and the Chancellery.

# Synthesis (1 page) — Where does the transparency record sit?

**Question:** Is Romania's SAFE governance bad, normal-for-Europe, or worse-than-peers?

**Bottom line:** Romania's SAFE transparency record is **worse than the standard civilian-procurement regime in Romania**, **roughly normal for European defence procurement under the national-security carve-out**, and **markedly worse than the better-disciplined peers (Czechia's NEN-driven publication, Poland's lower foreign-leakage exposure)**. But it is **not uniquely bad**, and it is **not principally a Romanian-political failure** — it is the predictable product of (a) the SAFE Regulation's hard 31 May 2026 deadline, (b) Romania's thin defence-industrial base forcing reliance on a small set of foreign primes, and (c) Romania's pre-existing defence-procurement carve-out from Law 98/2016, which long predates SAFE.

**Specific evidentiary calls:**

1. **Thread C (PSD influence-peddling).** No documentary finding has materialised. There are *real* facts (CMS engagement, Băcanu's advisory role, the firm's past Rheinmetall mandates) that support a *legitimate appearance-of-conflict* concern, but no DNA file, no Court of Accounts audit, and no whistleblower record exist. The accusation is **politically rational** (PSD is in the opposition with a CCR notification in motion) and **partially substantiated** (the appearance issue is undeniable), but it has not crossed the line from political accusation to documented finding. *Verdict: undetermined; lean toward "transparency-of-process problem, not corruption."*

2. **Thread D (Miruță's 30%).** The complaint is **specific, time-stamped, and structurally coherent** with the deadline-driven incentive system SAFE created. The €820 m PM-led reduction in the Rheinmetall package suggests the underlying claim is directionally accurate. But the headline 30% figure has not been validated contract-by-contract, and Rheinmetall has not responded. *Verdict: structurally credible, magnitude unverified.*

3. **Thread E (Law 4/2026 and the legal stack).** The SAFE-specific Romanian regime **does narrow transparency** in identifiable ways: urgency presumption by statute, no public RFP, classified award documents, no published evaluation reports, joint defence committees rather than plenary scrutiny, and a single individual (General Pleșa) sitting at three points in the chain. The OUG 38/2026 *constitutional* problem — adopted by a no-confidence-defeated government — is a separate institutional embarrassment regardless of whether the CCR upholds or strikes it down. *Verdict: documented narrowing of transparency; consistent with European defence-procurement norms but at the worse end of them.*

4. **Thread F (Romanian industry).** The industrial-offset story is the *strongest* positive — Pirochim Victoria, Mangalia, Automecanica Mediaș, UMB Piranha — *and* the most uncertain. The headline 50% commitment is enforceable in principle but untested in practice; the 3-month Pauliuc reports will be the first verification milestone. Compared with Poland's 89%, the offset is structurally constrained by Romania's industrial base, not by negotiation failure. *Verdict: substantially better than the maximalist critique ("the money all leaves Romania") and substantially worse than the official optimism ("60–70% local"); a realistic mid-case is 40–50% by value with significant capability gains.*

**One pivotal observation across all four threads:** the **Romanian transparency deficit is *not* primarily a deficit of *substantive* information that exists somewhere and is withheld**. It is overwhelmingly a deficit of *procedural* information about *how decisions were taken* — who chose Rheinmetall over Hanwha for Lynx, why CMS was selected for legal advice rather than the State Counsel's office, what justified the Mangalia OPV premium over Bulgaria's price, what the original RFI prices were before they "drifted." These are decisions that *could* be documented and published without compromising national security, but have not been. That is a political choice, made primarily by the Bolojan government and protected by the CSAT classification screen.

**Where Romania looks unusually bad:** the simultaneous-role concentration in General Pleșa (working group → evaluation committee designation → contract signature) and the adviser-firm-prime triangle (Băcanu / CMS / Rheinmetall). Both are *appearance* problems that European peers with stronger separation-of-functions norms would not tolerate.

**Where Romania looks normal-for-Europe:** the use of negotiation without prior publication, the CSAT-based gating model, and the classified award documentation — all are within OUG 114/2011's pre-existing scope and within EU Directive 2009/81/EC's permitted exceptions.

**Where Romania looks comparatively bad to specific peers:** Czechia's NEN-driven publication regime would publish far more on similar contracts; Poland's funds-stay-domestic structure converts most of the "transparency" question into an "industrial-policy" question (domestic political pressure substitutes for transparency mechanisms). Romania has neither model.

**Therefore the answer to the synthesis question is:**

> **Normal-for-Europe in legal form; worse-than-peers in actual practice, primarily because of (a) over-concentrated personnel roles, (b) under-published procedural records, and (c) Romania's structural dependency on foreign primes that the regime then has to defend rather than challenge in negotiation.**

> **Not bad in the "criminal-conspiracy" sense in which PSD's press releases frame it; bad in the "designed-to-make-accountability-hard" sense.**

The 3-month post-signature compliance reports mandated by the Pauliuc amendments are the first publicly anchored test point. If those reports are published in substantive form (not classified annexes) and reveal the local-content actually delivered versus committed, the transparency record will improve materially. If they are classified or stripped of programme-by-programme detail, the current critique will harden — and the next opposition government's audit of SAFE contracts (whenever that comes) will likely be politically devastating.

## What would change the verdict (forward markers)

Tracking the following observable events over the next 6–18 months should update the "worse-than-peers in practice" reading either way:

1. **CCR rulings (June–July 2026, expected)** on the Ombudsman's and Grindeanu's OUG 38/2026 notifications. Strike-down would force renegotiation of part of the package. Upholding would consolidate the regime but politically weaken the *outgoing* Bolojan camp.
2. **Court of Accounts intervention.** Either a *concomitent* audit announcement on SAFE in 2026 or a notable *absence* of one for 12+ months will be a strong signal of whether the institutional check is functioning.
3. **Pauliuc 3-month reports (August–November 2026, depending on contract signature dates).** Whether published, in what format, with what programme-level detail.
4. **DNA action or non-action.** A formal DNA file opening on the CMS-Băcanu-Gheorghiu nexus would move the Thread C verdict toward "documented finding." Continued silence keeps it at "political accusation."
5. **Cooperation Agreement texts.** Publication of the standard template (with or without commercial annexes) would be the single most important transparency unlock.
6. **Mangalia operationalisation.** If by end-2027 Mangalia is actually building OPVs with a stable workforce, the industrial-policy premium is justified; if it has slipped or pivoted to a different model (MSC as port operator, Desan as facility lessor), the SAFE premium was paid for an outcome that did not materialise.
7. **Rheinmetall Victoria construction progress.** The 2026–2029 construction timeline for the propellant plant is observable. Visible site progress in 2026–2027 is necessary to support the offset narrative.
8. **Comparative SAFE auditing.** If the European Court of Auditors or the European Defence Agency publishes cross-country SAFE-implementation comparisons in 2027–2028, Romania's relative position will become directly testable.
